A meal without down

John Glenn ate applesauce from a tube in space.[1] There is something wonderfully unheroic about that detail. Set aside, for a moment, the machinery around the astronaut and consider the less conspicuous work required by the mouthful. Getting food out of a container is one problem. Getting it through a body is another. The tube deals with the first. What deals with the second when the meal cannot simply be imagined falling towards the stomach?

NASA's historical account describes early space meals as demonstrations that people could eat and swallow in weightlessness.[1] That observation opens a question about movement. It does not establish everything about digestion during a long mission, but it is enough to unsettle the image of the digestive tract as a drainpipe. A drainpipe depends on a downward route. A living digestive tract has another means of propulsion.

The oesophagus propels swallowed material towards the stomach through coordinated muscular activity called peristalsis.[2] Think of a flexible sleeve being squeezed along its length. The wall participates in moving the contents. The sleeve is an analogy for motion, not a complete anatomical model. What changes in the reader's picture is the source of movement. The mouthful is carried by the work of tissue around it rather than left to find its own way down.

Movement alone, however, will not explain what becomes of lunch. Imagine dismantling a large object outside a building. Reducing it to pieces and carrying those pieces through a doorway are separate operations. A piece can lie outside after dismantling; an object already small enough can be brought inside without being dismantled. The distinction will matter every time the meal changes form.

Chemical digestion includes enzymatic hydrolysis, the breaking of particular molecular bonds through reactions involving water.[3] Absorption is the uptake of substances across the intestinal lining.[3] The first process changes molecules; the second changes which side of a living boundary they occupy. A substance can dissolve into a liquid and still remain on the gut side of that boundary. Conversely, a substance already in an absorbable form need not undergo every digestive step before uptake.

Consider an imagined meal containing bread, egg and oil. It is a deliberately modest meal for thought, not a dietary recommendation. Bread will let us follow starch, egg will let us follow protein, and oil will make the special problems of fat visible. The three questions are what moves the material, what changes its molecules, and how the resulting substances enter circulation. A useful answer needs verbs as well as organ names.

The five historical episodes accompanying this meal offer different views of those questions. Glenn provides the opening puzzle. Pavlov's experiments move the beginning of gastric work back before food reaches the stomach. Alexis St Martin and William Beaumont make the stomach accessible to observation while exposing the limits of visibility. Darwin approaches digestive chemistry through a leaf. James Lind brings the account to something an energy total cannot tell us: whether food supplies a substance needed for a particular bodily function. [1][4][7][9][15]

The meal is the thread joining these episodes. The aim is to follow it closely enough to notice when a familiar word changes its meaning. “Broken down” can describe a crumb made smaller or a molecule chemically altered. “Gone” can mean detached from a string, dissolved in fluid, or transported into blood. Each historical window helps separate such meanings. The route through the body becomes clearer as the words become more exact.

A mouthful can move, change and cross a boundary. Those are three different achievements.

Before the first mouthful arrives

In his Nobel lecture on digestion, Ivan Pavlov described experiments in which a dog's food was diverted before it could enter the stomach.[4] Gastric secretion nevertheless followed the act of eating in that preparation.[4] The animal ate, but the surgical arrangement prevented the swallowed food from completing its ordinary passage. This was sham feeding: the acts of eating were separated experimentally from the arrival of food in the stomach.

A workshop preparing its benches when a delivery is announced offers a useful comparison. Preparation can begin before the material arrives. To understand the arrangement, one must distinguish the signal from the delivery and ask how the signal reaches the place where work begins. Pavlov's preparation made a corresponding separation available for experiment. The stomach's response could be investigated while direct contact with the meal was withheld.

Pavlov reported that cutting the vagus nerves abolished the gastric response to sham feeding in the operated dogs.[4] Changing a connecting pathway changed the response. That gave the observation more force than a simple record that eating and secretion happened together. The means of making the comparison also remain part of the result: this was a surgically constructed experiment in dogs, not an unobtrusive observation of a human lunch.

Pavlov's lecture offered his own assurances about the animals' health.[4] Those assurances belong to the historical record, but they are not an independent welfare assessment. The same preparation that made the physiological distinction unusually clear required intervention in living animals. Its explanatory value and its ethical conditions must be considered together, without treating either as an answer to the other.

Human experiments have addressed related questions using different preparations. The abstract of a study by Konturek and colleagues reports that modified sham feeding stimulated gastric acid secretion in the people studied.[5] It also reports stimulation of pepsin secretion.[5] These are the main reported results available here; the full methods were not read. The study included distinct healthy and duodenal-ulcer groups,[5] so the findings should not be compressed into one universal fraction of anybody's digestive response.

A separate human study by Richardson and colleagues reported that distension, or stretching, of the stomach prolonged the acid response associated with modified sham feeding.[6] Preparation and arrival can therefore contribute to the same developing response. Pavlov's lecture itself acknowledged coordination through body fluids as well as through nervous tissue.[4] The explanation is larger than a command sent from the brain: it must account for what has been sensed, what has arrived and the conditions in the organ responding.

Return to the bread as it enters the mouth. Chewing breaks food into smaller physical pieces.[2] Saliva moistens the material being prepared for swallowing.[2] Salivary amylase begins the chemical breakdown of starch.[3] A smaller crumb, a moistened mouthful and a partly changed starch molecule are three different results occurring in the same place. The crumb can become easier to handle while some of its chemical structure is being altered.

Starch consists of linked glucose units.[3] This gives amylase a particular task: acting on a carbohydrate structure, rather than indiscriminately dissolving every kind of food. The familiar scissors analogy for an enzyme is useful when it directs attention to a specific cut. It becomes less useful if it suggests that a single universal tool handles starch, protein and fat equally.[3] For the bread, the important event is the beginning of a series of cuts. The individual glucose units needed later have not all appeared merely because the bread has been chewed.

The imagined mouthful has now acquired a history. Its size has changed, its wetness has changed and some molecular changes have begun. It can be swallowed while those changes remain incomplete. Glenn's puzzle concerned how it would move; Pavlov's preparation adds another dimension, showing how work farther along the route can begin before the mouthful gets there. The next window lets us look at what happens when it does arrive. [2][3][4]

Anticipation belongs in the explanation. It does not replace contact, chemistry or local control.

An opening and the person beside it

A way of seeing inside a stomach can look, in retrospect, like a scientific gift. Begin instead with the injury. In his account, William Beaumont dated the gunshot wound that injured Alexis St Martin to 6 June 1822.[7] He described a persistent opening communicating with St Martin's stomach.[7] The opportunity for observation belonged to the physician's research; the damaged body belonged to someone else.

Beaumont reported beginning his digestive experiments in 1825.[7] His account describes placing food through the opening and later retrieving it.[7] He could inspect changes that would normally remain hidden: the appearance of a piece of food, its consistency and the fluid around it. An opening enlarged the range of possible observations, but the investigator still had to decide what each observation meant.

In the first printed food-on-a-string experiment, Beaumont recorded pain and distress associated with the procedure.[7] The string was a means of recovering material for inspection, but its presence was also part of another person's bodily experience. The surviving description gives us that much. It gives no licence to add a reassuring conversation, enthusiastic agreement or private feeling that the record does not contain.

The relationship was not reducible to one adjective. Beaumont recorded St Martin's refusal of an operation intended to close the wound.[7] He also recorded St Martin leaving for Canada without obtaining the physician's consent.[7] The possessive perspective is Beaumont's. A departure described as an interruption of the investigator's work also shows that the person being studied could interrupt it. Neither episode settles the circumstances of every subsequent procedure.

Historian Alexa Green's study frames the relationship through the labour and service arrangements surrounding the research.[8] This account uses the study's abstract, not unexamined contract clauses. Its framing matters because participation occurs within a relationship, not simply at the moment when a measurement is taken. Scientific usefulness and freedom to refuse are different questions. The surviving evidence needs to be read for both.

The food on the string posed another problem. Beaumont recognised that bread could become detached before it had fully dissolved.[7] An empty string could therefore result from more than one process. The distinction is wonderfully concrete: something missing from the thread might still be elsewhere in the stomach. Watching a sample disappear from one location does not, by itself, reveal where its material has gone.

A different comparison reached further. Beaumont reported the breakdown of beef in gastric fluid removed from St Martin's stomach and kept outside the body.[7] The fluid could act after separation from the stomach wall. That observation challenged an explanation based only on the stomach grinding food. The comparison moved the question from “what does the organ do?” towards “what can material secreted by the organ do?” It still concerned visible breakdown, rather than a measurement of nutrient entry into circulation.

To connect that observation with the egg in our meal, we need a molecular scale. Proteins are chains of amino acids joined by peptide bonds.[3] Cutting such a chain can leave shorter chains, not necessarily a collection of separate amino acids. Pepsin participates in breaking proteins into smaller peptide fragments in the stomach.[3] The egg's protein has therefore begun a transformation whose products still require further processing. A visible piece of egg and an individual protein molecule are different objects, and their changes cannot be inferred from one another without care.

Parietal cells in the stomach produce hydrochloric acid.[3] The acid helps unfold proteins, exposing them to enzymatic digestion.[3] Acidic conditions enable the conversion of pepsinogen, a precursor, into active pepsin.[3] The acid and enzyme have related but distinct roles: changing the conditions and accessibility of the material is not the same as making every peptide-bond cut. Beaumont's extracted fluid was a working mixture, not a demonstration that one ingredient explains everything.

A bicarbonate-rich mucus coating contributes to protection of the stomach lining.[3] This belongs beside the description of digestive fluid. The stomach is living tissue containing an active chemical environment; the account must explain protection as well as breakdown. The stomach releases its contents into the small intestine gradually.[2] The egg's fragments will continue their journey rather than wait until the entire nutritional task has been completed in this one organ.

Beaumont's observations leave us with a way of asking better questions. If food changes in fluid outside the body, which properties of the fluid matter? If an ingredient is neutralised or removed, does the change continue? The next investigator approached those questions from a surface that seems, at first, far removed from a human stomach.

A change in what can be seen is not automatically a measurement of where a nutrient has gone.

The stomach that was not a stomach

In Insectivorous Plants, Charles Darwin described placing pieces of albumen on sundew leaves and comparing their fate with pieces kept on wet moss.[9] His chosen test material was coagulated albumen, the firm white of a hard-boiled egg.[9] The egg from the imagined meal has, in effect, supplied a second experimental question. Instead of following it through human organs, we can ask what happens to a defined piece of protein-rich material on a leaf.

Darwin acknowledged experimental help from Dr Burdon Sanderson.[9] The problem crossed fields: observing a plant's response led to questions about the chemistry of digestion. The leaf was useful because conditions could be changed and outcomes compared. It was neither a miniature stomach nor merely an attractive curiosity placed beside the human account.

Darwin's tests become especially revealing when their unsuccessful steps are retained. An initial, weaker alkaline treatment failed to stop the dissolution he was investigating.[9] In later trials, more thorough neutralisation prevented the albumen pieces from rounding off in the same way.[9] He then reported that adding weak acid allowed dissolution to resume.[9] Changing the acidity could interrupt the process and restore it. The first attempt had failed to change the condition enough; repeating the manipulation more effectively produced a clearer contrast.

That sequence establishes a role for the acidic environment. Another comparison helps show why acidity alone was an incomplete explanation. Darwin reported that comparable albumen cubes left in the weak hydrochloric-acid solution alone retained their sharp angles after seven days.[9] On the leaf, the material changed under conditions in which the weak acid by itself did not produce the same visible result. The secretion supplied something beyond acidity alone. The control gives the next inference its footing.

Darwin interpreted his results as evidence for a digestive agent functionally analogous to pepsin.[9] The analogy concerns what the agent did. It is not a molecular identification of human pepsin in a plant. The comparison with the egg's gastric processing is nonetheless useful: an acidic setting can support the action of a protein-digesting agent without the acid being the whole digestive system.

Darwin also described injury to leaves when stronger acid was used.[9] Conditions that enable a process can damage the organism if changed too far. The experiment therefore calls for a range of questions: did the treatment alter the working environment, act directly on the test material, or injure the tissue producing the secretion? Its logic is stronger because the albumen-only and leaf comparisons separate some of those possibilities.

Later biochemical work could ask more detailed questions about the active agents. Shizuko Amagase's investigation found acid protease activity, meaning protein-breaking enzyme activity under acidic conditions, in a preparation from Drosera peltata leaves and stems.[10] The paper also examined material from Nepenthes.[10] This was a different plant preparation from Darwin's particular leaf secretion. Its value is to carry the inquiry towards biochemical characterisation while keeping species and preparation visible.

The scissors analogy now has a fuller use. The material to be cut matters, the kind of cut matters, and the conditions under which cutting can proceed matter. A changed outcome after neutralisation can reveal a dependence on conditions. It cannot, by itself, supply every detail of the cutting agent's identity. Darwin's acid-only comparison and the later biochemical assays address different parts of that problem. [9][10]

For our meal, the leaf has clarified what it means to say that protein is digested. The location is not the mechanism: naming a stomach does not explain a reaction, and studying a reaction need not always require a whole stomach. We can now return to the human route with a more precise question. The egg's proteins are becoming fragments. What must happen to those fragments, and to the bread's starch, before the products cross into the body? [3][9]

A useful comparison reveals which part of a process still needs explaining.

At the boundary

The meal leaving the stomach is partway through its transformation. Following the bread and egg now requires a closer view of the tube's contents and its wall. The contents occupy the lumen, the space inside the digestive tube.[3] Crossing the wall is the next distinct event. Food can be far inside the outline of a person on a diagram while its products still await absorption across that living boundary.

The first part of the small intestine is the duodenum.[3] The pancreas delivers digestive enzymes to the small intestine.[2] Bicarbonate in pancreatic juice helps neutralise the acidic material arriving from the stomach.[3] Thus the egg's fragments move into a different chemical setting. The route is not a row of identical compartments: conditions suitable for one stage are changed as the material reaches another.

Folds, villi and microvilli enlarge the small intestine's absorptive surface.[3] Villi are projections of the intestinal lining.[3] Microvilli are much smaller projections on the absorptive cells themselves.[3] Imagine looking progressively closer: first the folded wall, then its projections, then the surface of an individual cell. The enlarged area gives the meal's products more boundary to encounter. What crosses still depends on the substance and the mechanisms available at that boundary.

For the bread, the chemical continuation is quite specific. Pancreatic amylase breaks starch into smaller carbohydrate fragments in the small intestine.[3] Enzymes at the intestinal brush border complete the breakdown of starch-derived fragments to glucose.[3][35] “Brush border” names the microvillus-covered cell surface just described.[3] The work begun in the mouth thus continues in the lumen and at the lining. A starch chain becomes smaller fragments, and those fragments yield individual glucose units. Structural and biochemical experiments with human maltase-glucoamylase linked a segment of its active site to a preference for longer sugar chains.[35] The experiment isolates part of the molecular machinery; it does not measure the digestion of a whole slice of bread.

The step across the cell's outer boundary is another process. Glucose enters intestinal absorptive cells through transport coupled to the movement of sodium ions.[3][33] Absorbed monosaccharides enter capillary blood that travels towards the liver through the portal circulation.[3] Digestion has supplied a substance that the transport system can handle; transport carries it across. A glucose unit does not enter blood merely because the last bond linking it to another sugar unit has been broken. A study of a family with glucose/galactose malabsorption identified an altered sugar transporter.[33] When the altered transporter was tested in frog oocytes, sodium-dependent glucose transport was lost.[33] The clinical observation and the functional test connect a problem with uptake to a particular piece of transport machinery.

Glucose is an absorbed monosaccharide, a single sugar unit.[3] The intestine also absorbs galactose[3][33], and it absorbs fructose[3]. These are other dietary sugars, not products that every starch chain must yield. The bread's starch route leads to glucose; the wider category of absorbable carbohydrate includes more than glucose.

A human experiment helps separate the cutting from the crossing. Gary Gray and Franz Ingelfinger infused human intestinal segments with sucrose or a comparison mixture of glucose and fructose.[30] During sucrose digestion, they found free glucose and fructose remaining in the fluid collected from the segments.[30] The broken-down sugar had not all crossed the lining. Measuring uptake required accounting for those products still on the lumen side. The test used sucrose, not bread starch, but it makes the distinction tangible: disappearance of the starting molecule and absorption of its products need not be equal.

The egg's continuation differs. Pancreatic protein-digesting enzymes, including trypsin, cut peptide chains further in the small intestine.[3] Peptidases at the brush border can release amino acids from peptide fragments.[3] The products are now smaller than the proteins that entered the stomach. The process can be followed as successive chemical changes rather than one unexplained leap from “egg” to “amino acids in blood”.

There is an important complication at the crossing. Intestinal cells can take up short chains of two amino acids[3][34] or three amino acids[3][34], as well as individual amino acids[3]. These absorbed short peptides can be broken into amino acids inside the cells.[3] In experiments expressing a cloned human intestinal transporter in cultured cells and frog oocytes, the transporter carried di- and tripeptides.[34] That result concerns transport; it does not itself demonstrate the subsequent cutting inside an intestinal cell. Some of the final cutting can occur after entry into an absorptive cell. Digestion and absorption are distinguishable processes, but their geography need not be a rigid sequence in which every cut happens outside before any crossing begins.

Another human experiment approaches that complication directly. Siamak Adibi perfused human intestinal segments with selected two-amino-acid peptides and comparison solutions of free amino acids.[28] Adibi interpreted the evidence as supporting uptake of the tested peptides in intact form as an important route.[28] The study concerned selected compounds and controlled solutions, not a complete egg meal. Its relevance is the contrast: the disappearance of a peptide from the lumen need not mean that it was first cut into free amino acids there. In the interpretation tested here, it can cross as an intact dipeptide.

Amino acids leaving intestinal absorptive cells enter capillary blood.[3] Cells can use amino acids to build new proteins.[3] The egg's original protein structure has been dismantled; its components can contribute to new structures. The earlier doorway analogy now includes a further step: what crosses the boundary can become material for construction on the other side. This is a use of absorbed food that an account concerned only with burning fuel would miss.

The oil presents a different physical problem. A triglyceride consists of glycerol joined to three fatty-acid chains.[3] The liver produces bile.[2] The gallbladder stores bile between meals.[2] Bile salts help disperse fat in the watery intestinal contents.[3] Lipase catalyses the breakdown of triglycerides.[3][37] Dispersion helps expose material; the enzyme makes chemical changes. Calling bile an enzyme would collapse those two jobs into one.

In assays with human pancreatic lipase, triglycerides were hydrolysed through diglycerides to monoglycerides.[37] Micelles are small molecular assemblies that help carry fatty acids and monoglycerides through the watery intestinal contents.[3] They are different from the fat droplets being digested.

Lipid components leave micelles before entering absorptive intestinal cells.[3] Within those cells, absorbed long-chain fatty acids and monoglycerides can be rebuilt into triglycerides.[3] These lipids are packaged into particles called chylomicrons for export.[3] The original arrangement in the intestinal contents is replaced by a new arrangement suited to the onward journey. Electron microscopy of rat intestinal cells after corn-oil feeding examined the assembly and secretion of nascent chylomicrons.[38] This gives a view of cellular packaging in that animal preparation; it is not a direct measurement of an ordinary human lunch.

A harbour offers a useful comparison. Goods arriving in one form are unloaded and repackaged before leaving on another route. The point is the change of package and route at a boundary. It helps distinguish the micelle approaching a cell from the chylomicron leaving it, rather than imagining the same parcel travelling intact through every stage.

Chylomicrons enter intestinal lymphatic vessels before reaching the bloodstream.[3] Short-chain fatty acids can enter portal blood rather than following the chylomicron route.[3] Thus the long-chain lipid pathway differs from the first route taken by sugars and amino acids, while even the category “fatty acid” contains exceptions. The three components of the meal converge in the body's uses without sharing one identical itinerary. In controlled rat infusions, Simmonds and colleagues measured the transfer of labelled fatty acids into thoracic-duct lymph.[36] Lipid infusion also increased the output of esterified fatty acids in that lymph.[36] That limited experiment joins a chemical change to a measured route; it does not establish one absorption fraction for every meal.

The portal route brings the bread's glucose to the liver, where storage adds another transformation. Glycogen is a polymer made from glucose units.[3] The liver can store glucose as glycogen.[3] The molecules released by dismantling one carbohydrate structure can therefore be assembled into another. During fasting, breakdown of liver glycogen can contribute to glucose production.[3][32] Absorption and immediate use need not occur at the same moment.

This storage step is measurable. Roy Taylor and colleagues used carbon-13 magnetic resonance spectroscopy to follow liver glycogen after a mixed meal in healthy volunteers.[27] They observed a rise in liver glycogen concentration after the meal, followed later by a decline.[27] The method allowed repeated observations of the store[27] rather than requiring its size to be inferred from the food's disappearance. The experiment used a specified liquid meal[27] and separate study protocols for related measurements[27]; it does not give one storage fraction for every person's lunch.

The meal has now acquired several destinations: components entering circulation, material available for new proteins, and glucose placed in a form that can be drawn on later. The bread has not simply vanished into an organ called the liver. Its starch has been cut, glucose has crossed a cellular boundary, and some glucose can enter a new molecular store. The egg and oil have arrived by their own series of transformations. What remains in the intestinal contents will have another history. [3][27][28][30]

Crossing the intestine changes both the form of the meal and the routes its components can take.

The fruit and the measure of recovery

The fifth historical doorway begins with an illness rather than an organ. In his 1753 treatise, James Lind described a comparison involving twelve patients with scurvy that he dated to 20 May 1747.[15] We encounter the event through his later account. The useful comparison must be reconstructed from what that account describes, rather than from the familiar label subsequently attached to it.

Lind reported keeping the patients on a common diet during the comparison.[15] He assigned different treatments to pairs of patients.[15] The published account does not describe random allocation.[15] It says that the two patients given seawater were particularly ill.[15] Shared conditions made some comparisons possible, while the described allocation left differences between the pairs that complicate interpretation. This was not a modern randomised trial merely because it involved groups receiving different treatments.

One pair received oranges and lemons.[15] The supply of fruit lasted six days.[15] Lind described one of those men as fit for duty after six days while residual signs of illness remained.[15] He described the other as recovered enough to help nurse the remaining sick.[15] Neither description establishes that both men were completely cured after those six days. They report meaningful but differently described changes in what the men could do and how they appeared.

The account also continues beyond that point. Lind reported that the first man became fully healthy before the ship reached Plymouth.[15] He mentioned continued use of a gargle during that later recovery.[15] The later report should remain beside the six-day description. It neither erases the earlier residual signs nor supplies a controlled comparison of every contribution to recovery. The timeline contains more than one endpoint, and the treatment history contains more than the initial fruit supply.

Lind's evidence concerned a food treatment. His reported comparison contains no measurement of blood vitamin concentration.[15] It does not isolate a single chemical constituent of the fruit.[15] A difference between treatments and identification of an active molecule are distinct achievements. The reported clinical change can be important even while its molecular explanation remains unavailable to that particular experiment.

Modern knowledge connects the episode to a specific nutritional function. Scurvy is a severe form of vitamin C deficiency.[26] Vitamin C is required for normal collagen formation.[26] Collagen is a protein used in tissues including skin, tendons, ligaments and blood vessels.[26] This makes the nutritional issue more concrete. The body requires more than material that can yield energy: it also requires conditions and substances that allow its own structures to be made and maintained.

A cell study approaches one part of that connection at closer range. Murad and colleagues reported increased collagen synthesis after prolonged vitamin C exposure in laboratory-grown human skin cells called fibroblasts.[16] The published abstract supplies the main result used here; it was not a trial of sailors or a demonstration that supplements prevent every infection. Its contribution is a narrower link between a specified substance and the production of a specified protein.

The egg in our meal provides the earlier half of a useful contrast. Protein digestion supplies amino-acid material that cells can use; the vitamin C example concerns a requirement for making a particular bodily product properly. Asking how much protein or energy a food contains does not automatically answer whether every such requirement has been met. Different nutritional questions call for different measurements.

Lind's account also changes how we listen to the word “recovery”. Fit for duty, able to nurse and fully healthy at a later point are not interchangeable descriptions. Each has a place on the timeline. The distinction resembles the one encountered at Beaumont's string: the meaning of a result depends on specifying what changed and where the observation stopped. Precision allows the episode to remain a human story while keeping the evidence attached to the outcome actually reported.

The fruit comparison therefore adds something the route through organs alone could not provide. It asks whether the substances arriving are adequate for the body's work. Following a nutrient across the intestine tells us how it becomes available. Understanding the consequences of its absence asks a different question about what the body needs to do with it.

Recovery has a timeline. A useful account says which change was observed, and when.

What remains and what leaves

The meal's products cross a living boundary, not a perfectly sealed partition with a few holes cut into it. A study of mouse intestinal preparations reported that deleting the tight-junction protein claudin-15 reduced sodium permeability between cells in the caecum and middle colon.[14] This was an experiment on a particular protein in a particular animal preparation. Its limited but useful contribution is to show why an absolute statement that nothing passes between neighbouring intestinal cells would be misleading. The boundary's properties are part of the physiology.

Water movement makes that point relevant to the ordinary meal. Fordtran, Rector and Carter measured water absorption in perfused segments of the human small intestine.[12] Adding glucose increased water absorption in the tested small-intestinal segments.[12] The jejunum is the middle region of the small intestine.[3] A later human jejunal study reported that glucose and fructose stimulated sodium absorption in chloride-containing test solutions.[13] These controlled measurements connect water movement with dissolved substances. They describe tested solutions and local segments, not a fixed share of every person's daily water uptake.

The watery mixture continuing along the gut also contains material whose story has not ended with the small intestine. Bacteria in the large intestine contribute to the breakdown of remaining material.[2] In a human study, Pomare and colleagues observed a rise in blood acetate after participants consumed the fermentable carbohydrate lactulose.[17] They also reported a slower acetate response after pectin.[17] Acetate in blood was the measured outcome[17], rather than simply the disappearance of material from the bowel.

Those results, available here through the published abstract, challenge the claim that all material escaping small-intestinal digestion simply leaves the body unchanged. They do not provide a complete measurement of energy gained or a common value for every type of fibre. The distinction matters because the large intestine can change the fate of some remaining material without making all fibres equivalent. The tested substrates produced different time courses in the measured blood acetate response.[17] [17]

The large intestine absorbs water.[2] This occurs after the small-intestinal water uptake already described. The division between organs is therefore not a division in which one organ alone owns the verb “absorbs”. Following the changing contents gives a more coherent picture than assigning a single exclusive job to each labelled shape.

Faeces include undigested material.[2] They also contain bacteria.[31] Material shed from the lining of the digestive tract contributes to faeces too.[2] In defecation, undigested material leaves the body in faeces.[3] The final material is consequently more than the recognisable remainder of the original meal. Its composition reflects events along the route as well as what was eaten.

Urine belongs to a different part of the account. The kidneys remove wastes from the blood in forming urine.[18] They also help regulate the body's water balance.[18] Following urine backwards leads to the handling of blood, whereas following unabsorbed meal residues leads back along the intestinal contents. Both are ways material leaves the body, but they answer different questions about its prior journey.

Much of our imagined meal has already crossed the intestinal boundary and become available for other work. Cellular breakdown reactions, collectively called catabolism, can provide energy for making ATP.[3] ATP supplies energy for processes including muscle contraction.[3] The name ATP identifies a molecule involved in coupling the release of chemical energy to cellular work; it is not another name for a piece of food. Digestion, uptake, storage and eventual use belong to one connected account without becoming one event.

We can now widen the question. One meal helps supply a body that moves food, maintains boundaries, builds tissue and stores material for later use. What energetic possibilities were available as such bodies evolved? The route through organs leads naturally to a question about the costs of those organs, but the evidence must change with the scale of the question.

An energetic bargain or a larger budget

Leslie Aiello and Peter Wheeler proposed the expensive-tissue hypothesis as an explanation linking the energetic demands of large brains with a reduction in the digestive tract.[19] The proposal asks how one energetically costly organ might expand while the demands of another diminish. It is compelling partly because it offers a specific mechanism to investigate rather than merely observing that brains require energy.

A household budget makes the central assumption visible. With spending power held fixed, increasing one expense requires a reduction somewhere else. With a larger income, that inference no longer follows. The analogy supplies two different questions for biology: how is a total allocated, and can the total itself change? Organ size, organ expenditure and the whole body's expenditure must then be measured or estimated rather than treated as interchangeable entries in the account.

The original proposal connected a more readily digestible, higher-quality diet with the possibility of supporting a smaller digestive tract.[19] Processing the meal more effectively could, in the hypothesis, change the anatomical investment needed to obtain its benefits. The phrase “higher quality” concerns the energetic argument, not a moral ranking of present-day meals. In their reply to commentators, Aiello and Wheeler did not insist that gut reduction had to initiate brain expansion.[19] Their own discussion therefore leaves room for a more complicated sequence than the familiar version in which one organ shrinks and another automatically grows.

A broad comparative test gives genuine contrary evidence. Navarrete and colleagues reported no negative association between brain mass and digestive-tract mass after accounting for fat-free body mass in their mammalian comparison.[20] This is the main abstract-level result used here. It challenges a simple universal brain-for-gut exchange. Its scope is a comparative pattern; it does not establish that every possible energetic trade-off must be absent in every species or setting.

Experimental evidence from fish addresses another scale. In a guppy selection experiment, lines selected for larger relative brains developed smaller guts.[21] The larger-brained lines also produced fewer offspring in the first clutch examined.[21] An advantage in the study's learning task was detected in females but not males.[21] The experiment joins a potential benefit to particular costs. The relevant outcomes are gut size, the first clutch and performance in one task, not a single general score called evolutionary success.

This is a case in which the budget comparison earns its place. Selection changed one trait, while other measured traits changed with it. The result makes a trade-off possible to discuss concretely in these fish. Extending that account to human ancestors would require further evidence. The mammalian comparison and the fish experiment need not be forced into a verbal contradiction: they examine different populations under different conditions and ask different versions of the allocation question.

The total budget also has empirical support as a variable. Pontzer and colleagues measured total energy expenditure in living humans and other apes using doubly labelled water.[22] Their tracer method followed labelled body water over several days to estimate whole-body energy expenditure.[22] Their analysis found higher adjusted total energy expenditure in humans than in the other apes studied.[22] A larger total gives the evolutionary account another possibility besides a fixed-budget swap between two organs.

Total daily energy expenditure includes expenditure across daily life, whereas basal metabolic rate concerns expenditure at rest, after food absorption and in a temperature-neutral environment.[22][3] They are different measurements, not different names for one quantity. The ape sample included animals living in captivity or sanctuaries.[22] The study reused some previously collected ape measurements.[22] Its result is a living comparison with a documented sampling history, not a direct record of what happened in an ancestral population.

A more recent human study deliberately asked a broader tissue-allocation question. Bezerra and colleagues used latent-variable modelling in a sample of seventy South Asian women.[25] The model represented proposed nutritional investment in brain and lean body tissues using measured brain, organ and muscle variables.[25] In this use, a latent variable is an inferred quantity represented through several measurements, rather than something measured directly by one instrument.[25]

The study did not directly measure gut size.[25] The authors explained that measuring the moving gut with MRI would require a movement-suppressing drug, so they examined a broader brain-versus-other-tissues hypothesis.[25] The reported 95 percent confidence interval for the brain and lean-tissue estimate included zero.[25] The authors described their results as inconclusive.[25] An interval spanning zero leaves a zero effect within the reported uncertainty under that model; the result does not resolve a negative trade-off. The study's own chosen scope and uncertainty belong to its contribution, rather than being flaws discovered by renaming it a direct gut experiment.

Cooking connects the energetic question back to the food itself. Carmody and colleagues compared cooked and raw foods in short feeding experiments with adult male mice.[23] In the meat experiments, the mice lost body mass under all treatments.[23] The loss was smaller with cooked meat.[23] The improvement was relative: less loss, not absolute gain. That outcome supports a consequence of processing under the experiment's conditions without establishing a universal caloric bonus for cooking every food.

The egg provides a human comparison with a different endpoint. Evenepoel and colleagues studied egg-protein digestion in five people with ileostomies.[24] An ileostomy brings the end of the small intestine through the abdominal wall to an opening.[29] The researchers collected material leaving the small intestine after labelled egg-protein meals.[24] They reported higher true ileal digestibility for cooked than raw egg protein.[24] Here the outcome concerns the estimated fraction digested by the end of the small intestine[24], rather than a change in body weight. The published abstract supports the comparison used here; neither this small clinical sample nor the mouse experiment supplies a universal whole-diet calorie conversion.

The evidence now has several forms: a proposed allocation mechanism, a broad comparison that fails to find one predicted pattern, an experimental trade-off, a larger measured total budget, an intentionally broader human model and processing studies with different outcomes. These are not five votes to be counted for or against one slogan. They ask different questions. A stronger account must preserve those differences while considering how the mechanisms might interact. [19][20][21][22][23][24][25]

The conditional conclusion is substantial enough. Changes in food and its processing could have altered the energetic possibilities available to evolving bodies. Establishing which changes caused which others in human evolution requires more than demonstrating one possible mechanism. The expensive-tissue hypothesis remains useful because it generates questions about allocation. Evidence for a changing total budget adds another question. Together they replace an inevitable sequence with a research problem whose alternatives can be tested.

A plausible energetic bargain is a hypothesis to test, not a receipt from the evolutionary past.

Returning to the table

Return to the mouthful with which the essay began. The surprising thing about Glenn's applesauce was that the ordinary direction of falling could be set aside while a person still swallowed. What first looked like a puzzle about motion has led to a larger discovery about the way we describe food. “The meal” is a convenient name for something that gradually ceases to have one location, one form or one destination. [1]

The bread's starch can be followed through cuts to smaller fragments, through the release of glucose, across the intestinal lining and towards use or storage. The egg's protein can be followed through peptide fragments and amino acids into material available for new proteins. The oil's long-chain components undergo a change of molecular packaging before taking the lymphatic route. None of these paths is fully described by saying that the food disappeared. Their meaning lies in the transformations and crossings already traced. [3][34][35]

The most revealing moments have been boundaries of different kinds. A swallowed mouthful meets a moving muscular wall. A piece on Beaumont's thread crosses from visible presence to an ambiguous absence. Darwin's albumen meets conditions that can be changed and compared. Lind's patients cross different thresholds of recovery at different times. The intestinal lining supplies a literal boundary, while the energy studies require a distinction between what a particular experiment measures and the history we hope to explain. [2][7][9][15]

There is a quiet connection between the chemistry and the historical accounts. In both, the familiar object must be taken apart without losing track of its components. The meal cannot remain a single undifferentiated substance if we want to understand its fate. A famous experiment cannot remain a single undifferentiated “discovery” if we want to understand its evidence. What was done, what was observed and what was inferred have to remain connected but distinguishable.

That is also what gives the school diagram behind Matens väg, the path of food, its deeper interest. Its labelled organs are places at which different verbs become necessary: move, change, absorb, rebuild, distribute, store. Following those verbs turns an outline of the body into an account of work. The scientific stories make the work memorable because each one reveals a different part of it.

A meal therefore offers more than a route to memorise. It offers a way to practise attention: to a material's form, to the side of a boundary it occupies, to what a comparison can show, and to the point where an explanation remains conditional. The work of a good story is to make those distinctions memorable without making them disappear.
