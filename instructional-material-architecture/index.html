One Road, Many Handrails

The best learning resource may not be one book or many choices. It may be a shared route through rich knowledge, with carefully placed supports that appear when they are useful and disappear when they are not.

August 14, 2026 · source-audited edition · ≈ 33 min read

Decision first

What should the teacher build?

Start with one authoritative, knowledge-rich route. Add only three types of optional layer: access, temporary guidance and extension. Assign a layer because of a known barrier, an observed error or demonstrated mastery. This is an evidence-informed design hypothesis, not a directly tested package. In the default route, students should meet the same goals, key concepts, causal sequence, central representations and assessment logic.

Minimum for week one: one start page, one core explanation and representation sequence, one shared checkpoint, one reusable support and one extension task. Teach this before building more.

Do not begin by producing three complete material versions labelled easy, ordinary and difficult. This is an implementation recommendation, not a directly tested treatment comparison. Do not give novices a library of equivalent sources and ask them to diagnose which one is best. Do not mistake a short text for a simple learning environment. A short text can be conceptually fragmented, while a longer resource can be easy to navigate if its route is explicit.

On the first morning of a new science unit, three kinds of complexity enter the room. There is the complexity of the body itself: molecules become nutrients, nutrients cross membranes, blood transports them, cells transform them. There is the complexity of learning: unfamiliar words and invisible processes must become connected models. Then there is the complexity created by school: links, folders, versions, optional films, support sheets, extension tasks and the question that can consume a lesson before biology has even begun: Which one am I supposed to use?

The first two arise from the subject and the process of learning. Much of the third is created by the instructional system and can therefore be redesigned.

The wrong question is “one material or many?”

Counting resources is tempting because it is visible. One booklet looks simple. Eight links look complicated. Yet the number alone tells us little. A single all-in-one resource can require students to search, filter and decide what matters. Four representations can form one coherent explanation if the teacher sequences them and explicitly shows how they correspond.

The useful distinction is not between one and many. It is between one coherent route and many competing routes. It is also between representations that perform different intellectual jobs and duplicate resources that leave the student to determine which version is authoritative.

A photograph, an organ diagram and a molecular model can be three windows into one idea. Three unrelated websites can be three doors into three different corridors.

This reframing matters because the research rarely compares the packages teachers actually face. There is no strong body of experiments assigning Swedish grade 8 science classes to a comprehensive textbook, a simple booklet, a menu of equivalent resources and a common core with modular support. Evidence instead concerns components: prior knowledge, worked examples, text and image integration, representation, autonomy, scaffolding, differentiation and implementation. Any complete recommendation is therefore a synthesis. It should be treated as a design hypothesis with strong parts and uncertain joints.

Cognitive load is not a synonym for “difficult”

Cognitive load theory begins from a practical constraint. When students process novel information, working memory cannot coordinate an unlimited number of interacting elements. Knowledge stored in long-term memory changes this calculation because a knowledgeable learner can treat a familiar pattern as one organised unit rather than as many separate details.[1] This is why the same page can be manageable for one student and bewildering for another.

The theory does not justify removing every difficulty. Some complexity belongs to the subject. To understand digestion, a student must eventually coordinate structure, substance, movement and function. Removing those relationships could lower the apparent burden while also removing the understanding. The instructional task is to reduce effort that does not contribute to learning, such as searching among files or matching a distant label to a diagram, while preserving the effort needed to build a causal model.

Critics of cognitive load research have also noted conceptual and measurement problems. Researchers do not always separate kinds of load cleanly, and a rating of mental effort is not a transparent window into the mechanism that produced a test result.[2] A material should therefore not be declared cognitively superior because it looks clean or feels easy. Learning, delayed recall, transfer and navigational behaviour all matter.

Productive difficulty has a place

In a study of eleventh-grade science, students were randomly assigned to work first on ill-structured or well-structured Newtonian kinematics problems. The groups who struggled with ill-structured problems later outperformed the comparison condition on individual near- and far-transfer measures.[15] This is an existence proof, not a licence to replace teaching with unguided discovery. It shows that uncertainty can be productive in a carefully designed sequence.

The design target is thus not minimum effort. It is useful effort with minimal avoidable friction. A student comparing two models of nutrient absorption may be doing valuable intellectual work. A student comparing four documents to discover which one the teacher intends is not.

The fourth model is the most defensible starting point, but its name can conceal two very different designs. In the strong version, the core is a rich, cumulative explanation and the layers change access, guidance or depth. In the weak version, the core is a simplified minimum, while complexity is placed in an optional extension that only confident students reach. The first preserves a common entitlement to knowledge. The second can quietly create tracks without naming them.

A minimum viable first unit

The practical advantage of a shared route is that the teacher can make a consequential decision now without pretending to know every future need. The unit does not need to begin with a finished ecosystem of alternatives. It needs an authoritative route, a small set of reusable supports and a method for discovering what to change. That is enough to teach the first lessons and learn from them.

Build this, then teach
  1. One start page. It shows the unit route and always marks the current step.
  2. One recurring lesson grammar. Goal, short explanation, central representation, worked example, core task, exit ticket.
  3. One knowledge-rich core per subtopic. Brief enough to navigate, rich enough to preserve necessary relations.
  4. One integrated representation sequence. For example everyday object, organ, tissue, molecule and system consequence.
  5. One fading sequence. Complete example, partially complete example, independent task.
  6. One language scaffold. Definitions, word parts, sentence frame or concept map linked to the same core.
  7. Known access alternatives. Text-to-speech, captions or adapted layout where they remove a real barrier.
  8. One extension bank. Comparison, transfer, explanation and model evaluation.
  9. One common assessment logic. Shared key questions and criteria, regardless of access route.

"One" in this list means one authoritative location for each shared element, not necessarily one document. The start page may point to a text, a teacher-led model and a practical task. The distinction is architectural. Students should not have to infer which source defines the vocabulary, which task is required or which explanation will matter in assessment. Each shared element has an owner, and every additional item has a declared relationship to it.

The first core text should be written or selected only after the causal route is clear. Begin by listing what students must be able to explain at the end. Then work backwards. Which relationships make that explanation possible? Which representations reveal those relationships? Which words are indispensable? This prevents the common failure in which a short text becomes a pile of isolated definitions. A resource is simple to navigate when it makes relationships visible and decisions predictable, even if the science remains appropriately demanding.

For the opening lesson, a blank body map can elicit evidence of prior knowledge without requiring a permanent track. Ask students what they believe happens to a familiar food after it enters the body. Use the responses to identify missing links, competing models and language needs. The diagnostic is not a ranking ritual. It is routing information.

For the first core explanation, choose a causal chain short enough to hold together but rich enough to be scientifically meaningful. Introduce a small number of indispensable terms before asking students to coordinate them. Keep the same colours, arrows and definitions across teacher presentation, student material and assessment. Six experiments using engineering and biology materials found that integrating mutually dependent sources helped when learners would otherwise have to combine them mentally, but not when each source could be understood alone.[6]

At the checkpoint, use one or two questions that distinguish different difficulties. A student who cannot define absorption needs a different support from a student who knows the definition but cannot explain why surface area matters. Routing by the observed error is a more instructionally specific heuristic than assigning a fixed identity such as "weak." Reviews of scaffolding emphasise this contingency: support should respond to current performance and later fade.[13]

After the core is secure, choice can widen. Students can apply the shared model to protein, fat or carbohydrate, compare two dietary claims, explain a graph or evaluate a misleading diagram. The sequence moves from common orientation toward increasing independence. The important variable is not whether the student experiences choice, but what intellectual work remains common across the options.

A Week in the Chemistry of Life

Imagine the first five lessons as a rehearsal of the whole architecture. The purpose is not to finish digestion in a week. It is to establish a rhythm that students can recognise and the teacher can reuse. Familiar routines reduce the need to explain the logistics of learning anew each lesson. That does not make the intellectual work repetitive. It frees more attention for the changing scientific problem.

Lesson one: reveal the starting models

The class begins with one question on the board: "Where does a piece of bread go, and what happens to it on the way?" Students draw and annotate alone for three minutes, compare with a partner and then examine two anonymous examples selected by the teacher. One example treats digestion mainly as transport. The other includes transformation but places it in the wrong organ. The teacher does not immediately label students or distribute different booklets. The drawings identify the conceptual obstacles that the common explanation must address.

The central representation is a simple route through mouth, stomach, small intestine, blood and cell. It is intentionally incomplete. At the end, every student answers the same exit question: "Why can a starch molecule not simply move unchanged from bread into a body cell?" A student may dictate the answer or use a sentence frame, but the required relationship remains the same. Access and knowledge are separated.

Lesson two: make an invisible change visible

The start page sends everyone to the same short explanation of large molecules, digestion and absorbable units. Beside the relevant sentence sits a diagram, not on another page. The teacher works through one example: starch is a large carbohydrate; digestion produces smaller sugar units; those units can cross an intestinal surface; blood can then transport them. A second example contains blanks at the final two links. A third asks students to construct the full chain for protein.

This is the handrail sequence in miniature. Students who need it can retain the completed example. Students who demonstrate the relation can skip repeated support and answer a transfer question. The primary study cited here concerns mathematics, so this biology design transfers a principle rather than an effect estimate.[4] Evidence on fading likewise supports a transition from examples toward problem solving, especially for near transfer.[5]

Lesson three: connect scales

Students now encounter three representations because the science requires them: an organ view of the small intestine, a tissue view of a villus and a simplified cell-membrane view. The teacher controls the sequence. Students first locate the same boundary in each image, then trace one molecule across it. Only after correspondence is established do they receive all three together.

This is not a menu. Nobody chooses an image based on preference. Each representation performs a complementary job, and translating between them is part of the learning goal. Evidence on multiple external representations suggests that several can support STEM learning when their relations are adequately supported, but effects are heterogeneous.[8] The practical implication is to teach the translation rather than merely display the images.

Lesson four: use difficulty diagnostically

Students receive a partially incorrect explanation of absorption. Their task is to find and repair two errors. The problem comes before the teacher's final consolidation, but it is bounded: the relevant core and diagrams are available, the number of errors is known and the class later compares solutions. This preserves productive struggle without turning the lesson into unsupported discovery. A primary study in eleventh-grade Newtonian kinematics shows that initial failure on complex problems can later be productive for near and far transfer, but its conditions differ substantially from this grade 8 biology lesson.[15]

The errors also route support. If a student confuses digestion with absorption, the teacher points to the transformation and crossing steps in the shared chain. If another student understands both but cannot connect villus structure to function, the teacher opens the tissue-level model. A third student may be ready to compare the absorption of glucose and fatty-acid products. There are different next moves, but not three disconnected courses.

Lesson five: widen choice without losing the destination

Students choose one of two contexts: explain what happens to carbohydrate after a meal, or explain what happens to protein. Both require location, transformation, absorption, transport and cellular use. The choice changes the object of explanation but preserves the shared causal structure and success criteria. A digital-media study found that two types of limited, partly artificial choice could support learning and autonomy. That is modest evidence for bounded choice, not evidence for a large resource menu.[10]

The week ends with a common delayed prompt rather than a satisfaction survey alone: "A student says that food travels directly from the intestine to every body cell. Improve the explanation." The response shows whether students can coordinate the shared route after the immediate lesson context has faded. The teacher also records which supports were used, which navigation questions recurred and how much preparation the layers required. The material has now produced local evidence about both learning and maintainability.

The shared spine: what must remain common

A spine is not simply a file. It is the stable logic of the unit. For chemistry of life and the human body, it might be the journey from food to usable matter and energy:

  1. Food contains substances with different molecular structures.
  2. Digestion changes large molecules into units that can be absorbed.
  3. Absorption moves those units across an intestinal surface.
  4. Circulation transports materials between organs and cells.
  5. Cells transform materials, including in cellular respiration and biosynthesis.
  6. Regulatory systems maintain useful ranges rather than static conditions.

Every lesson, support sheet, image and assessment question should know where it belongs on that route. The common elements should include learning goals, vocabulary definitions, major causal chains, the numbering of tasks, central models and criteria for success. This reduces the chance that support versions introduce different terminology or that students receive different explanations of the same mechanism.

A single start page can carry the route without carrying all the content at once. It needs to answer three operational questions: Where do I begin? What do I do now? How do I know that I am finished? The page can reveal the next stage while still making the whole unit visible as a map.

The core should standardise orientation, not standardise every learner's experience.

Science needs many representations, not a resource buffet

Science learning often depends on moving between levels that cannot be seen at once. A sandwich is visible. Starch molecules are not. The small intestine is an organ with folds, yet absorption is also a cellular event and a molecular transport process. A graph of blood glucose is neither an organ nor a molecule, but it represents change produced by both.

A meta-analysis of STEM learning found small average benefits from using more than two external representations in some comparisons, with substantial heterogeneity and a strong role for support in connecting them.[8] The review also contained subgroup results that warn against assuming uniform benefit, especially for secondary learners. The responsible conclusion is not “more is better.” Multiple representations can serve complementary scientific functions and can improve learning when their relationships are taught.

Place mutually referring text and diagrams close together. Six experiments using electrical engineering and biology materials found benefits of integration when the sources had to be mentally combined, but not when each source could be understood alone.[6] Signal the part currently under discussion. Segment a complex process into meaningful phases. In two multimedia experiments, learner control over the pace of a segmented narrated animation improved transfer but not retention.[7] The principle is not to chop every explanation into fragments, but to let a learner coordinate one meaningful change before the next arrives.

A sequence for nutrient absorption

Begin with the ordinary scale: a meal enters the digestive tract. Move to the organ scale: the small intestine provides a large interface. Move to the tissue scale: villi and epithelial cells shape that interface. Then move to the particle scale: digestive products cross membranes and enter blood or lymph. Finally return to the whole body: circulation makes local absorption relevant to distant cells.

Do not show every representation simultaneously merely because they are available. Introduce them in a purposeful order, then ask students to map correspondence: Where is the glucose in this picture? Which arrow in the organ diagram corresponds to the change in the graph? What does the molecular model explain that the photograph cannot?

Redundancy also needs precision. Experimental work has tested conditions in which identical spoken and written words can help rather than hinder multimedia learning, contradicting any blanket rule that repeated verbal information is always harmful.[9] Narration, animation and identical full text can still compete under other conditions. A concise label integrated into a diagram may help. The question is not whether information overlaps, but whether each representation performs a necessary function for these learners and this task.

Guidance should behave like a handrail

A handrail is most valuable at a steep or unfamiliar section. It does not carry the walker, and it need not continue across level ground. The expertise reversal literature captures this changing relationship between support and knowledge. A recent meta-analysis reported that higher guidance favoured lower-prior-knowledge learners on average, while lower guidance favoured higher-prior-knowledge learners, with important moderators and less certain conclusions for some younger groups.[3]

This is an argument for adjustable support, not for producing a separate book for every learner. A short diagnostic question can determine whether a student begins with the fully worked model, a partially completed version or an independent explanation.

Primary research in mathematics has shown that carefully chosen sequences of worked examples and problems can produce more than memorised procedures, including recognition of when a procedure applies.[4] Biology is not identical to algebra, so the result should not be transplanted as an effect estimate. The transferable design idea is to make expert reasoning visible. A complete example might trace starch from food to blood glucose while explicitly naming each transformation, location and mechanism. The next task can remove the final causal link. The next can remove two. Evidence on fading supports smoother transition chiefly for near transfer, not a guarantee of deep or distant transfer.[5]

Scaffolding is often mistaken for an easier worksheet. Reviews define it more dynamically: support should be contingent on what the learner currently needs, fade as competence grows and transfer responsibility to the learner.[13] A permanent “weak student version” does not satisfy that definition merely because it contains hints.

Choice works best inside a structure

Autonomy and structure are not simple opposites. In observations of 133 public high-school classrooms and reports from 1,584 students in grades 9 to 11, autonomy support and structure were positively correlated and both predicted observed behavioural engagement; only autonomy support uniquely predicted students' self-reported engagement.[14] The study is correlational and does not test material menus, but it undermines the idea that clarity and agency must trade off. A clear route can create the competence needed for meaningful choice.

A digital-media study reported that learning-relevant and irrelevant feigned choices could support learning, autonomy and intrinsic motivation, alongside lower perceived difficulty.[10] The manipulation was narrow and partly artificial. The result supports testing bounded choice, not asking students to select among many complete resources.

Learner-controlled hypermedia research gives the complementary warning. Open navigation can produce disorientation, distraction and overload, with outcomes depending on prior knowledge and self-regulation.[11] The safest default is therefore a recommended path with limited, meaningful decisions.

Good choice

After learning the common model, students choose whether to trace carbohydrate or protein through it. Both options require the same causal reasoning. Or they choose whether to express the explanation as a labelled sequence diagram or a tightly structured paragraph, provided both formats reveal the same knowledge.

Poor choice

Students are told to learn digestion and are offered a textbook chapter, two films, a podcast, a simplified site and a slide deck without a default or explanation of function. The choice is not about intellectual direction. It is about discovering the hidden design of the lesson.

Access without dilution, depth without gatekeeping

Some learners require alternative access formats or accommodations. The evidence reviewed here does not evaluate Universal Design for Learning as a package, nor the effectiveness of specific accessibility supports. Those questions require a separate evidence base. Here the design principle is normative: an access route should preserve the intended scientific knowledge.

A student who listens to a text should not receive a different causal model from the student who reads it. A glossary should clarify the language needed for the subject, not erase the subject language. A chunked version should preserve connections across chunks. An additional example should reveal the same underlying structure. Access changes the route into the knowledge. It should not quietly lower the destination.

The same principle protects students with high prior knowledge. If every learner must process detailed support long after it has become redundant, the common core becomes a ceiling. Students who demonstrate the core should be able to move directly to comparison, explanation, model criticism or transfer. Extension should not mean twenty more repetitions. It should mean a harder intellectual relation.

Every extra version becomes a maintenance promise

Teacher workload is not an afterthought because implementation quality is part of educational effect. A theoretically excellent design that cannot be maintained will drift. The secondary differentiation literature identifies preparation time, resources and organisational support as important constraints, while the achievement evidence remains sparse and heterogeneous.[12] Survey and mixed-method work also suggests that differentiated instruction can carry stress as well as professional satisfaction.[17]

No experiment establishes that one core plus modules saves a fixed number of hours. The workload argument is a systems inference. If three complete versions have separate instructions, diagrams, tasks and answer keys, each correction can require three edits and three checks. If a common explanation has one optional glossary and one optional worked example, the shared part is corrected once.

There is an important exception. Several high-quality, ready-made resources may reduce teacher production compared with building a universal core from scratch. A six-year national survey report tracks how many materials teachers report using and how they report modifying them, but the locally available abstract does not establish which combination is best or most common.[16] The practical rule should therefore be “curate before creating.” A second resource earns its place when it performs a defined function better than a new homemade version could.

Control growth with resource roles

Every item should have one declared role: core, representation, access, practice, extension or assessment. If its role cannot be named, it is probably duplication. Do not add a new support after one isolated difficulty unless the need is documented. If many students need the same support, repair the core. Collect changes during the unit and revise once afterwards rather than creating live copies whose relationships nobody can remember.

The sustainable unit is not the one with the fewest files. It is the one in which every file has a job and only one file is authoritative for each shared element.

A test for every extra resource

A sustainable architecture needs a gate, because useful-looking additions accumulate easily. Before adding a film, second text, worksheet or interactive, write one sentence beginning: "This resource exists because..." The sentence must name a function and a learner problem. "Students may like it" is not specific enough. "The animation shows the direction of molecular movement that the static organ diagram cannot show" is.

Then ask whether the resource is required for everyone, an access alternative, temporary guidance, practice, extension or assessment. A required representation belongs in the teacher-controlled sequence. An access alternative should be available without forcing a student to announce a diagnosis. Temporary guidance should have an exit condition. Extension should require deeper relation, transfer or critique. Assessment should use the same concepts and causal logic as the core.

The final question is about maintenance. What shared element does this resource duplicate? If a definition changes, how many places require correction? If the teacher cannot answer, the new item creates hidden debt. The problem is not file count by itself. Ten small, clearly routed supports may be easier to maintain than two sprawling documents with overlapping explanations. Authority and dependency matter more than quantity.

Existing commercial or open resources can pass this test. A textbook chapter might serve as the authoritative reference while a teacher-made start page marks the required route through it. A high-quality animation might become a sequenced representation. A concise existing text might become an access route if it preserves the same relations. Survey researchers have explicitly tracked the number of materials teachers report using and how they modify them, but that evidence does not identify one universally efficient combination.[16] Curating is therefore not a compromise. It is the central professional task.

Three warning signs suggest that the material system is beginning to sprawl. First, students repeatedly ask which source is the real one. Second, the teacher corrects the same explanation in several places. Third, assessment questions rely on language or representations that appear in only one optional branch. Each sign points to a different repair: clarify authority, consolidate duplication or restore alignment.

A resource earns its place by doing a job that the shared route needs, not by being good in isolation.

When the default should change

A shared route with optional layers assigned in response to observed need is a starting architecture, not a universal commandment. It can fail when the range of prior knowledge is so wide that one pace repeatedly wastes time for some students and withholds needed continuity from others. It can also fail when an access need requires a genuinely different sequence rather than a small adaptation. In those cases, a temporary or sustained alternative route may better meet those learners' needs.

The decision should be based on observable mismatch. If a student repeatedly demonstrates the core before instruction and learns little from the common sequence, acceleration may be warranted. If another cannot access the central explanation even with language, layout and example supports, a more substantial alternative may be necessary. The expertise reversal literature gives a reason to avoid forcing redundant guidance on knowledgeable learners, but does not prescribe a single grouping system.[3]

An alternative route should still declare what remains common and what changes. Perhaps the same destination is reached through more explicit language preparation and additional worked examples. Perhaps an advanced route reaches the common checkpoint earlier and then pursues a connected investigation. Perhaps an accommodation changes the modality and timing of response. Naming these differences protects against the drift from alternative access into alternative entitlement.

Fixed labels deserve particular caution. The secondary differentiation evidence is promising in parts but sparse, heterogeneous and sensitive to how adaptation is implemented.[12] A label such as easy, medium or hard can become an identity even when it began as a practical folder name. Prefer temporary routing language: "use the model with prompts for this task," "move to the comparison task after the checkpoint," or "listen while following the same diagram." The student is not the support.

There are also topics in which multiple sources are part of the intellectual goal. Students may need to compare competing models, evaluate claims or detect how representation changes an argument. In such a lesson, suppressing alternatives would impoverish the subject. The teacher can still reduce navigational load by selecting the sources, stating why they differ and providing common questions for comparison. Several sources become one designed inquiry rather than an unstructured search.

The criterion is therefore not conformity. It is coherence. Different paths can be coherent when their purpose, destination and relation to one another are visible. One path can be incoherent when it hides priorities or mixes explanations without signalling the change. The architecture should expand only when local evidence shows that the default route cannot responsibly carry the learners who are using it.

The smallest responsible decision

For a teacher facing the first unit, the immediate decision can be written on one page. At the top sits the explanation every student should eventually command. Beneath it are the representations everybody will study in a fixed order. Beside it are three drawers: access, guided practice and depth. The drawers are not opened all at once. The teacher opens one because a known barrier, an observed error or demonstrated mastery gives a reason.

This resembles a well-organised laboratory more than a supermarket. A laboratory may contain many instruments, but the procedure does not invite a novice to choose any tool at any moment. The task determines which instrument appears, the teacher explains its function and unnecessary equipment stays off the bench. Variety remains available without becoming the student's navigational burden.

The teacher can begin with existing assets. The concise PDF may provide orientation or an access route. A fuller chapter may supply the authoritative explanation and reference detail. Teacher-written bridge texts can connect the two and repair gaps in vocabulary, causal order or representation. The crucial move is to assign roles before students see the materials. If both texts explain the same idea differently, choose which wording is canonical and explain the other's purpose. If the short text removes a relationship required for assessment, do not call it the core merely because it is easier to read.

Nor should every potential support be built in advance. Prepare the supports that are predictable from prior teaching, documented needs and accessibility requirements. For everything else, create a placeholder in the architecture, not the resource itself. A blank "extra example" slot takes seconds. A polished alternate booklet can consume hours and may never address the actual difficulty. The first teaching cycle should discover demand before the system commits to supply.

This also changes how revision feels. Instead of asking whether the entire unit was good, the teacher can ask which layer failed. If students reached the correct page but misunderstood digestion, the core explanation or instruction needs repair. If they understood orally but could not enter the text, the access layer needs repair. If confident students completed repetitive work without learning, the routing or depth layer needs repair. If everybody asked which document to open, the architecture failed before the science began.

Such diagnosis protects teacher energy. It turns a vague pressure to differentiate everything into a finite set of design decisions. Research on differentiated instruction records both perceived benefits and stress, while not directly comparing these material architectures.[17] The promised workload reduction is therefore not an established effect. It is a testable local expectation: shared components should reduce duplicated maintenance, while targeted layers should concentrate effort where it changes access or learning.

The first unit is consequently allowed to be incomplete. It must be coherent, accessible enough to begin and honest about what is not yet known. After teaching, the teacher can keep the layers that solved recurring problems, fold widely needed support into the core and discard the rest. The architecture grows like a path worn by actual footsteps, not like a road network drawn before anyone has entered the landscape.

Test the architecture without creating a research project

Local evidence cannot prove a universal causal effect, but it can reveal whether a design functions in these three classes. The pilot should be deliberately small. Record only information that can change a decision.

Decide the stopping rule in advance. A new support should not become permanent simply because it was created. Keep it if it solves a recurring, identified problem without fragmenting goals or assessment. Repair the core if many students require the same addition. Remove an option that is rarely used and does not answer a documented need.

The honest boundary of the evidence

The recommendation is stronger than intuition but weaker than a direct comparative trial. Much cognitive load research comes from mathematics, multimedia and higher education. Studies of differentiation use inconsistent definitions and often researcher-made measures. Choice experiments usually manipulate one bounded decision rather than the navigation of an entire unit. Teacher workload studies are largely observational, self-reported or practice-based.

Consequently, no exact number of materials can be scientifically prescribed. “At most two choices” is a useful restraint in some novice situations, not a law. “One common core” is a governance principle, not proof that every student should receive identical media. “Less cognitive load” is not automatically a virtue if it is achieved by removing the relationships students need to understand.

The synthesis cautions against two extremes. A single undifferentiated text should not be assumed to meet every access need, and a large open menu should not be assumed to constitute effective differentiation. Between those extremes lies a disciplined design: common intellectual direction, multiple purposeful representations, responsive supports and a teacher who remains the architect of the route.

The goal is not to give every student a different road. It is to make the important road legible, then place the right handrail where someone actually needs it.

For the first unit, the most responsible decision is therefore modest. Build one strong route. Add only the supports whose functions can be named. Preserve the full scientific story. Watch how students enter, navigate, remember and explain. Watch how much time the system demands from its teacher. Then revise once, from evidence rather than anxiety.
