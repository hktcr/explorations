The screen was not the strategy

What school digitalisation achieved, why mass device rollouts so often disappointed, and what the evidence really says about books, teacher capacity, and the arrival of AI

August 2, 2026≈ 31 min read30 bibliographic records checked

Sweden and many other countries put computers and tablets into pupils' hands before schools had built a reliable way to turn access into learning. That much of the backlash gets right. The larger conclusion, that digital tools were tried and found educationally harmful, does not survive a careful reading of the evidence. General device programmes often produced small or null average effects. Specific tools sometimes helped. Paper retains real advantages for some reading, and phones create distinctive attention costs. The neglected variable was not technology alone, but the institutional capacity around it. AI is now exposing the same gap at greater speed.

The short answer

The popular story contains a large piece of truth. Sweden and many other countries expanded access to laptops, tablets, learning platforms, and digital teaching materials faster than schools developed a reliable pedagogy for them. A device was visible, countable, and easy to procure. The less visible work, redesigning lessons, giving teachers time to practise, building subject specific support, evaluating results, and deciding when not to use a screen, was slower and much less even.

But the stronger version of the story, that schools tried digital technology and discovered that it was bad for learning, is not a good summary of the evidence. Studies of one device per pupil often find no average improvement in conventional test scores. Some find small benefits, some find small harms for particular groups, and many show that the devices were used mainly for writing, searching, and administration rather than for transformed teaching. At the same time, controlled studies and research syntheses find positive effects from particular applications, especially when they provide practice, feedback, simulation, or scaffolding and are integrated into teacher led instruction.[6][7][15][18]

Some criticisms do concern the medium itself. Reading an expository text on paper can yield a small comprehension advantage over reading the same text on a screen, particularly under time pressure or when scrolling is required. Personal phones can divert attention even when they are not intended as teaching tools. Poorly designed software can add cognitive clutter, surveillance, advertising, or friction. Very young children have needs that do not map neatly onto screen based activity.[22][23][25]

Those findings justify selective limits. They do not justify treating a phone notification, a digital novel, speech synthesis, a geometry simulation, a spelling program, and a teacher's interactive model as one exposure called screen time. The educational question is not simply whether a screen is present. It is what the pupil is doing, what cognitive work the tool performs, what it displaces, and how the teacher uses the information or possibilities it creates.

Was the rollout too fast?Often yes, if “too fast” means that procurement outran pedagogy, support, governance, and evaluation.
Did devices lower results?No general causal verdict is available. Average effects of one to one programmes are often small or null, with important variation.
Was competence development missing?It is one of the best supported explanations, but time, leadership, task design, infrastructure, and incentives also matter.
Should schools retreat?Targeted removal can be sensible. A general analogue turn discards useful tools and mistakes a category for an intervention.
The most defensible diagnosis is not that Sweden invested too much or too little in “digitalisation.” It invested asymmetrically: heavily in access, unevenly in the human and institutional capacity that could turn access into learning.

The claim behind the reversal

In March 2023, the Swedish government sent the National Agency for Education's proposed digital strategy out for consultation with an unusually blunt framing. Digitalisation had, it said, in some places proceeded in a “hasty and ill considered” way. Printed textbooks, pens, and paper had been pushed aside. The government also stressed that not all digitalisation was bad.[8] The distinction mattered, but it was soon overshadowed by a clearer political story: more books, fewer screens.

That story responded to real anxieties. Swedish pupils had experienced a long period of uneven results. Teachers and parents saw phones competing for attention. Schools sometimes bought systems that created more administration without improving instruction. Digital textbooks could be harder to navigate than printed ones. Technology companies routinely promised personalisation, engagement, and efficiency without evidence proportional to the claims. A correction was unsurprising.

Yet a political correction can combine several different diagnoses. One says that certain media are inferior for certain tasks. Another says that unrestricted devices create distraction. A third says that teachers were asked to implement a reform without sufficient preparation. A fourth says that commercial procurement was weak. A fifth says that digitalisation displaced proven practices without showing that the replacement was better. These claims can all be true at once, but they imply different remedies.

If paper supports sustained reading, provide paper. If phones disrupt attention, restrict phones. If a platform consumes teaching time, replace it. If teachers lack time and subject specific training, changing the object on the desk will not solve the underlying problem. The danger in the broad term digitalisation is that it turns distinct mechanisms into one villain, and then invites one broad reversal.

There is no single digital tool

A useful analysis begins by disassembling the category. At least six technologies have been bundled together in school debates.

A seventh category has now arrived. Generative AI can explain, translate, draft, quiz, imitate feedback, and produce plausible errors. Unlike a book displayed on a screen, it can perform part of the cognitive task that the pupil was supposed to practise. This makes the old lesson relevant, but not sufficient.

“Screen time” is sometimes a useful public health shorthand. It is a poor theory of instruction. Two pupils can spend twenty minutes with identical tablets while one passively scrolls through unrelated video and the other tests how changing a coefficient transforms a graph. The physical medium is the same. The attentional structure, knowledge demands, feedback, and learning opportunity are not.

What Sweden actually did

Swedish school digitalisation did not begin with a single national switch. Municipalities and independent providers made different purchases at different times. Many schools adopted one device per pupil. Learning platforms became routine. National policy encouraged adequate infrastructure, digital competence, and the use of digital tools, while local implementation determined what these words meant in classrooms.

By the early 2020s, access was no longer the central national problem. The National Agency for Education's follow up of the 2017 to 2022 strategy described access as generally good, although two in ten teachers still said they lacked the digital tools needed to support all pupils. The larger weaknesses concerned systematic planning, assessment of teachers' development needs, evaluation of professional learning, and pupils' ability to search for and assess information critically.[4]

The OECD's 2026 diagnostic review of Sweden gives the same pattern sharper definition. Swedish pupils reported extensive access and intensive use. Many were satisfied with the resources available. Yet frequent device use in mathematics lessons was associated with lower performance, distraction was common, and access did not automatically translate into strong digital literacy. The OECD repeatedly cautioned that these relationships are not simple causal estimates. Pupils who use a device frequently may differ in prior achievement, school context, or the support they need.[1]

The review also found an institutional gap. Thirty nine percent of Swedish pupils attended schools whose principals said mathematics teachers had not received professional development on integrating digital resources into teaching. This sat beside apparently reassuring perceptions: most principals and many pupils thought teachers had sufficient technical and pedagogical skills. The two measures are not contradictory. A teacher can be competent with routine systems and still lack sustained development in how a simulation, feedback system, or collaborative tool should change a particular sequence of mathematics lessons.[1]

A separate 2026 analysis by the National Agency for Education makes the gap difficult to dismiss. Around seven in ten school IT coordinators said insufficient teacher digital competence obstructed the use of digital tools. More than six in ten pointed to insufficient planning time. Four in ten reported an inadequate shared vision, and roughly four in ten inadequate pedagogical support. Four in ten schools lacked a clear plan or vision. Effects of professional development were not evaluated at three in ten schools, and changes in pupil outcomes were used as an indicator at fewer than one in ten.[2]

These are survey and administrative findings, not proof that a particular training course would raise test scores. They nevertheless show that the “missing competence” account is not an excuse invented after disappointing results. The organisations expected to implement digitalisation reported missing time, support, direction, and evaluation while the reform was in progress.

The problem also appears in pupil outcomes. In the 2023 International Computer and Information Literacy Study, four in ten Swedish eighth graders did not reach the level the agency described as important digital competence. Swedish performance was around the EU and OECD average, and many pupils struggled to assess the credibility of online information.[3] A highly connected school system had not made advanced digital judgement universal. Access and competence were never the same variable.

What the Swedish studies found

The strongest Swedish studies do not produce a morality tale. An IFAU evaluation of one computer per pupil in lower secondary school found no average effect on national test results in Swedish, English, or mathematics, and no clear effect on the transition to upper secondary education. There were indications of weaker results among pupils whose parents had less education, and tablets may have been less favourable than laptops for some Swedish outcomes. The main result, however, was not a large collapse. It was a costly reform with little detectable average academic effect.[6]

An IFAU study of one device per pupil in middle school reached a similarly restrained conclusion. It found no effect in mathematics or English and a tendency toward a small improvement in Swedish. Teachers experienced greater possibilities for individualisation, but pupils did not report more motivation or greater variety. Much of the use remained ordinary searching and writing. The machine had changed the infrastructure more than the instructional model.[7]

A 2019 Schools Inspectorate review helps explain why. In 27 lower secondary schools, digital tools were often used for general functions such as writing, information search, and learning platforms. Use for subject specific problem solving, simulation, or deeper conceptual work was more limited. Technical familiarity did not automatically become pedagogical capability. Twenty of the 27 schools needed stronger leadership from the principal to ensure that digital tools supported the curriculum.[5]

These findings are compatible with several interpretations. Perhaps general devices have little effect because most useful school tasks can already be done in other ways. Perhaps the measures missed gains in writing process, digital production, accessibility, or information literacy. Perhaps benefits and harms cancelled each other. Perhaps weak implementation prevented a promising intervention from being tested. A null average does not tell us which of these mechanisms dominated.

It also matters what was evaluated. A one to one programme is an offer of capacity, not a defined lesson. In a medical trial, researchers specify the substance and dose. “A laptop per pupil” is closer to giving every clinic a laboratory and then estimating the average effect on health. The infrastructure may enable valuable practices, useless practices, or no change at all. The average result is important for judging the procurement, but it cannot reveal whether every practice made possible by the equipment is ineffective.

The world experiment that was not one experiment

The international history reinforces the Swedish pattern. Early digital school policy often assumed a chain that looked almost automatic: access would increase use, use would increase engagement, and engagement would increase learning. Each link proved conditional.

One of the clearest cases was Peru's One Laptop per Child programme. A randomised evaluation across 318 rural primary schools sharply increased computer access and use. It improved pupils' general computer skills and produced some inconclusive evidence of gains in general cognitive ability, but found no effect on mathematics or language achievement. The programme had delivered the device it promised. It had not delivered a corresponding transformation of instruction or curriculum.[16]

The OECD's 2015 report Students, Computers and Learning created a memorable international backlash. Across PISA data, moderate school computer use was often associated with better performance than no use, while very frequent use was associated with worse results. Countries that had invested heavily in information and communication technology had not, on average, produced appreciable improvements in reading, mathematics, or science. The report's core interpretation was not that computers cannot help. It was that technology had often been added to existing pedagogy without mastering the teaching practices needed to use it well.[11]

Later PISA analyses preserve the inverted U shape in many settings: some purposeful use can coexist with stronger results, while high intensity or distracting use correlates with weaker results. These are observational relationships. High use can be a response to weak performance, and countries differ in curriculum, software, selection, teacher capacity, and home use. PISA can identify a warning signal. It cannot randomly assign national digital strategies.[12]

UNESCO's 2023 Global Education Monitoring Report reached a similarly conditional verdict after reviewing evidence across countries at very different income levels. Technology can expand access, support some forms of teaching, and assist learners with particular needs. Evidence is scarce relative to the scale of procurement, products change faster than evaluation, and benefits can disappear when use becomes excessive or when qualified teachers and basic conditions are absent. Simply distributing computers has repeatedly failed to improve learning on its own.[14]

That history supports the charge of haste. It does not support a universal negative effect. The world did not conduct one experiment called digitalisation. It conducted thousands of interventions, from device donation in schools without reliable electricity to sophisticated tutoring systems, from replacing a printed worksheet with a PDF to allowing pupils to model a chemical reaction. Collapsing their results produces an average whose policy meaning is limited.

The evidence that complicates the backlash

If digital technology were inherently educationally harmful, more specific research would consistently point in the same direction. It does not.

A review of rigorous field experiments by Escueta and colleagues found mixed results for simply expanding access to hardware. It found more encouraging evidence for computer assisted learning programmes that target instruction and practice, while emphasising that programme design and context determine results.[15] This distinction between access technology and instructional technology is one of the most stable findings in the field.

Meta-analyses of one to one laptop programmes have reported positive average effects in several subjects, although the number of strong causal studies is limited and programmes vary widely.[17] Broader syntheses across decades also find a positive average effect of educational technology, accompanied by very large variation.[19][20] An average positive effect does not make every purchase wise, just as a null result for a hardware programme does not make every application useless.

The pattern becomes clearer when researchers examine functions. A meta-analysis of digital tools in secondary mathematics and science found positive effects overall. Simulations and intelligent tutoring applications tended to perform better than generic hypermedia. Effects were descriptively larger when technology complemented other teaching rather than replacing it, but teacher training was the only instructional moderator that reached statistical significance.[18] Another review found modest positive effects for educational applications in K to 12 mathematics.[21]

These syntheses have familiar limitations. Published studies can overrepresent successful pilots. Researchers often support implementation more intensely than a school system can. Comparison groups differ. A tool may improve a short test closely aligned with its exercises without producing broad transfer. Still, the totality of evidence contradicts the proposition that digital media have one fixed effect.

A useful distinctionHardware programmes ask whether providing a general capability changes average outcomes. Instructional programme studies ask whether a defined tool, task, and teaching sequence changes a defined outcome. The second question is narrower, but it is also closer to how learning happens.

Some benefits are also easy to omit from conventional tests. Text to speech can make a curriculum accessible. A shared document can expose revision as a process. A simulation can make an invisible mechanism manipulable. A pupil can receive immediate low stakes feedback rather than waiting days. These possibilities are not automatic benefits. They become benefits only when the activity serves a learning goal and the teacher can interpret what follows.

When paper really does have an advantage

The case for print is stronger than a nostalgic preference. A 2018 meta-analysis comparing reading on paper and digital devices found a small average paper advantage in reading comprehension. The disadvantage for screens was more pronounced for expository texts, under time constraints, and when readers had to scroll. It was not a universal inability to understand words on glass.[22] A separate meta-analysis reached a broadly similar conclusion and suggested that readers can be overconfident about comprehension on screens.[23]

The mechanism may involve navigation, spatial cues, scrolling, habits of skimming, or the device environment. The evidence does not prove that paper possesses a mysterious cognitive property. It shows that, for many sustained and informational reading tasks under current conditions, paper is often the safer default.

Yet even this result depends on what is placed on the screen. A 2022 meta-analysis of children's reading found no general screen disadvantage for narrative texts. Across its included studies, multimedia and interactive functions were associated with better comprehension on average.[24] The difference between a static page, a scrolling PDF, and a carefully designed interactive story is educationally meaningful.

A sensible school can therefore use printed books for extended reading without making print a constitutional principle. It can preserve handwriting and physical materials where they serve learning, while using digital search, composition, accessibility, data, or simulation where those functions add something. “Book or screen?” is often less useful than “Which representation, for which phase of this task, for which pupil?”

The special case of phones and distraction

Personal smartphones deserve separate treatment because they are not merely small school computers. They contain social relationships, games, news, and systems engineered to regain attention. Their default environment competes with the teacher.

A study of school phone bans in England found improved examination results after bans were introduced, with gains concentrated among lower achieving pupils. The average effect was modest, but its distribution mattered for equity.[25] Other observational research and PISA responses link digital distraction with lower performance, although causal effects depend on enforcement and context.[1][12]

This is good evidence for attention policy. It is not evidence against a teacher controlled laptop used for a defined task. A blanket phrase such as “screens in school” makes the phone ban stand in rhetorically for every digital practice. That leap is attractive because both objects glow. It is weak because the mechanisms differ.

Restrictions also need implementation. A phone placed in a bag but repeatedly checked can remain mentally present. A laptop with unrestricted messaging can recreate the same problem. Conversely, a classroom device configured for a task can reduce it. The policy target should be competing attention and off task affordances, not light emitting surfaces.

The missing layer

The competence explanation is well supported, but the word competence can become a convenient abstraction. It is not enough to offer a short course on which buttons to press. Effective digital teaching requires several forms of knowledge and several organisational conditions.

The OECD's current work on teacher preparation stresses that digital competence must be connected to curriculum, pedagogy, assessment, and professional collaboration, not treated as a generic technical add on.[26] Its broader review of digital education argues that access alone does not guarantee learning and that successful digitalisation requires pedagogical as well as technical solutions.[13] Comparative work on digital education likewise treats governance, infrastructure, institutional capacity, and professional learning as enabling conditions rather than side issues.[30]

Professional development is also not a one time inoculation. A teacher may need to see a model lesson, plan with a colleague, test the activity, inspect pupil work, and revise it. Subject differences are fundamental. The useful digital knowledge of a music teacher is not the same as that of a history or special education teacher. Generic inspiration sessions rarely build this repertoire.

Time is therefore part of competence development, not an optional resource beside it. When more than six in ten Swedish IT coordinators point to insufficient planning time, they are describing a design flaw in the reform. Schools cannot add new forms of teaching while protecting every existing schedule, administrative demand, and assessment routine.[2]

Leadership matters for the same reason. Principals and providers determine whether tools are selected for instructional reasons, whether teachers can learn together, whether ineffective licences are stopped, and whether evidence travels between classrooms. Without this layer, “teacher autonomy” can mean that individual teachers carry the full risk of a system level purchase.

Too much investment, or the wrong investment?

The proposition that Sweden should have invested more rather than less is plausible only after the object of investment is specified.

More devices would not have solved a reform already characterised by broad access. More licences would not have supplied planning time. More inspirational training would not have created subject knowledge or implementation support. Sweden did spend too much on some systems that were weakly evaluated, poorly integrated, or quickly obsolete. A critique of procurement is compatible with a call for greater investment in teaching capacity.

The stronger formulation is that Sweden underinvested in the complementary assets required to earn a return from hardware: sustained professional learning, curriculum aligned resources, technical and pedagogical support, time for redesign, local evidence, and the ability to stop using a tool that did not work. Economists would recognise a general pattern. Installing a new technology without reorganising work often produces disappointing productivity because the visible capital is only one part of the change.

This account also explains why advocates and critics can both point to genuine evidence. Advocates see positive effects from well designed instructional tools and accessibility. Critics see null results from broad device programmes, distraction, and print advantages. Both can be right because they are describing different interventions.

There is a harder possibility. Even with excellent training, a general purpose device may add little to some lessons. Professional development should not be a doctrine that every tool can be redeemed. A capable teacher needs permission to conclude that a printed book, physical manipulatives, oral discussion, or direct explanation is better. The goal is not maximum digital use. It is better learning with a deliberate media mix.

What the Swedish reversal gets right

Sweden's analogue turn responds to three evidence based concerns.

First, schools need high quality printed textbooks. A printed book can provide a stable sequence, overview, and shared reference. Restoring access after weak procurement or excessive reliance on fragmented digital material is not technological reaction. It is basic instructional infrastructure. The government's strengthened provisions on access to teaching materials addressed a real gap.[10]

Second, early childhood deserves particular caution. In 2025, Sweden removed the preschool curriculum requirement to use digital tools, required an analogue environment for children under two, and urged restraint for older preschool children.[9] Young children need language rich interaction, movement, play, manipulation, and responsive adults. A requirement to use a device can be as doctrinaire as a ban. Removing the requirement does not prove that every carefully mediated digital activity is harmful, but it restores professional discretion around a weakly justified mandate.

Third, attention should be protected. Phone restrictions and classroom routines have stronger mechanistic justification than an undifferentiated campaign against digital tools.

The reversal overreaches when these valid claims become evidence that the previous investment as a whole was a failed educational theory. Sweden did not consistently test the strongest version of digitally supported teaching. In many classrooms, it tested ordinary teaching plus a general purpose device. Removing the device may remove distraction and friction. It may also remove accessibility, feedback, creation, simulation, and the digital literacy pupils still need.

A national policy can also create a new compliance ritual. If “more screens” was once treated as modern, “fewer screens” can become the new visible indicator of quality. Neither count reveals whether pupils are reading deeply, receiving useful feedback, reasoning mathematically, or evaluating sources.

The equity problem runs in both directions

Digital policy has at least two equity problems. The first is access: some pupils lack devices, connectivity, quiet space, or support outside school. The second is quality of use: pupils with similar access may learn very different things depending on teachers, tasks, prior knowledge, and home resources.

The first divide made one to one provision morally compelling. A school could ensure that every pupil had the basic instrument. But equal hardware did not equalise the ability to search, judge sources, regulate attention, solve technical problems, or use a tool to learn. The Swedish ICILS result, four in ten eighth graders below an important competence level, is evidence that the second divide remained.[3]

The distribution of harms matters too. The English phone ban study found the largest gains among lower achieving pupils.[25] The Swedish lower secondary study found indications of weaker device effects for pupils whose parents had less education.[6] Unstructured digital freedom can benefit pupils who already have strong self regulation while imposing a larger attentional cost on those who do not.

But withdrawal can also widen gaps. Affluent families can buy tutoring, software, connectivity, and AI subscriptions. Pupils with dyslexia, motor difficulties, limited Swedish, or visual impairment may depend on digital access. If school stops teaching disciplined, critical use, it does not make the digital world disappear. It transfers instruction to households with unequal capacity.

The equity question is therefore not access versus limits. It is how to provide universal access to useful functions while placing stronger structure around uses that generate unequal distraction or require prior skill. Equality can require both provision and constraint.

AI is the same warning, but a different technology

The parallel with generative AI is already visible. Use is spreading faster than shared professional knowledge, institutional rules, and evaluation.

In a 2026 National Agency for Education report based partly on a nonrepresentative respondent panel, almost eight in ten participating teachers said they had used AI in their work, compared with four in ten in 2024. Four in ten allowed pupils to use AI, up from fewer than two in ten. Around half said they needed substantial support. More than a third mainly received support from colleagues, while only a little over one in ten pointed to the principal or provider. In the nationally designed TALIS 2024 survey, 31 percent of Swedish lower secondary teachers reported using AI, compared with an OECD average of 36 percent, and about a quarter identified AI as their greatest professional development need.[27]

This resembles the earlier reform: adoption first, collective capacity later. It would be easy to repeat the hardware error by buying licences, counting logins, and calling the result innovation. It would be equally easy to repeat the backlash error by responding to cheating and unreliable output with a general ban that leaves pupils and teachers to learn privately.

AI is not merely another screen. A laptop normally presents or records a pupil's work. A generative model can produce the work, imitate an explanation, adapt a quiz, translate a passage, or invent a citation. It changes the boundary between assistance and substitution. It also introduces new problems of data protection, bias, authorship, environmental cost, market dependence, and assessment validity.

For that reason, professional development must go beyond prompt tips. Teachers need to understand what the systems can and cannot know, how to verify output, how a task changes when production becomes cheap, which uses preserve desirable effort, and when no AI should be present. UNESCO's competency frameworks for teachers and pupils emphasise a human centred approach, ethics, foundational knowledge, pedagogy, and continuing professional learning.[28][29]

Assessment may require redesign. If a take home essay no longer shows who did the reasoning, schools can use supervised writing, oral defence, process evidence, iterative critique, or tasks in which AI output itself becomes an object of analysis. The answer is not necessarily to abandon extended writing. It is to restore a valid connection between the evidence collected and the capability being judged.

The productive question is not “How do we get AI into every classroom?” It is “Which learning problems can this capability help solve, under what safeguards, and what human capability might it displace?” That question should be asked before mass procurement, not after.

A more useful policy agenda

A policy that follows the evidence would stop counting devices and start specifying educational functions. It would ask what problem is being solved, what the tool replaces, what teachers need to use it, and which outcome will reveal whether it helped.

Choose defaults by task

Use printed books as a strong default for sustained expository reading when digital features add little. Protect handwriting, discussion, physical materials, and memory practice where they serve the goal. Use digital tools when search, revision, accessibility, modelling, feedback, collaboration, or creation provides a real advantage. A default simplifies practice; it need not become a ban.

Separate attention policy from technology policy

Restrict personal phones and noninstructional notifications. Configure school devices for the task. Teach pupils how attention is captured and how to regulate it. Evaluate whether restrictions change behaviour and learning, especially for pupils most vulnerable to distraction.

Fund the invisible infrastructure

Every major procurement should include protected planning time, subject specific professional learning, pedagogical and technical support, accessibility review, and a budget for evaluation. If these costs make the programme look expensive, that is useful information. The earlier price was artificially low because essential implementation work was shifted onto teachers.

Evaluate functions, not brands

Schools need evidence that travels. Instead of asking whether a particular platform is popular, examine whether a defined activity improves a defined capability compared with a plausible alternative. Measure workload and opportunity cost as well as pupil outcomes. Publish negative results so neighbouring schools do not repeat the same purchase.

Build AI capacity before AI dependence

Teach verification, model limitations, data protection, bias, authorship, and appropriate assistance. Redesign assessments before they become invalid. Pilot narrow uses with teacher involvement, then stop, adapt, or expand them according to evidence. Do not make school systems dependent on a vendor before they know what capability they are buying.

None of this promises a technological transformation. It offers something less dramatic and more credible: a way to discover which tools improve which forms of learning, and to abandon the rest without confusing caution with retreat.

The lesson was never inside the machine

The first era of school digitalisation was built around an object that politicians could photograph: a laptop or tablet in every pupil's hands. The current reaction is built around another photograph: a child with a printed book. Both images compress the difficult part of education into the medium on the desk.

The evidence permits a harder conclusion. Sweden and many other systems often moved too quickly from access to expectation. They treated infrastructure as if it were an instructional reform. Broad device programmes then produced the kind of result that should have been expected: useful new capacities, new distractions, altered workflows, and small or uneven average changes in traditional attainment.

The failure was not only insufficient training. It was an incomplete theory of change. Teachers needed subject specific knowledge, time, leadership, support, reliable systems, and permission to mix media. Governments needed to evaluate purchases and distinguish learning functions from modern appearances. Some tools would still have failed. Others could have produced more value.

Removing the least useful or most distracting technologies is not an admission that progress was a mistake. It is what a learning system should do. Turning that correction into a general flight from digital tools would repeat the original error in reverse: once again allowing the object to substitute for pedagogy.

AI makes the choice immediate. Schools can distribute access and hope that learning appears. They can ban the unfamiliar and let unequal private use continue. Or they can make the slower investment that the previous reform exposed: building collective professional judgement before a tool becomes ordinary.

A computer is infrastructure. A textbook is infrastructure. An AI model is infrastructure. None contains a pedagogy. The educational return comes from the knowledge, routines, relationships, and institutions built around it.
