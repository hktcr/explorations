Support without passivity

How teachers can combine clear instruction with genuine student agency

It is tempting to see four kinds of student. That temptation is the first problem this article tries to solve.

Passenger, Achiever, Resister and Explorer are more useful as names for temporary classroom modes. The same student may move between them during one lesson. A pupil may explore confidently in practical science, resist a writing task, become a passenger during a long explanation, and act as an achiever when a familiar test format appears. The names should therefore direct the teacher's attention towards the present relationship between support, purpose and responsibility. They should never become identities, diagnoses or seating plans.

The deeper question is not whether teachers should choose structure or agency. It is how structure can make agency possible without quietly replacing it. Students need access to knowledge, examples, language, tools and feedback. They also need chances to judge, choose, monitor, revise and take responsibility. If support is removed too early, independence can become abandonment. If support remains after it has served its purpose, teaching can produce accurate compliance without intellectual ownership.

Research does not provide a simple formula for finding the perfect balance. The evidence reviewed for this article is both useful and limited. It offers a bounded picture of when problem solving before instruction has helped, when it has not, and how teacher autonomy support is associated with several forms of student motivation. It does not validate the four modes. It does not prove a causal mechanism joining structure to agency. It does not tell a Swedish lower secondary teacher exactly how much support a particular pupil needs on Tuesday morning.

The initial research question was framed partly through cognitive load theory. The final evidence set did not retain a directly verified cognitive load theory source. This article therefore does not use that theory to explain motivation, agency or the four modes. Questions about task complexity and prior knowledge remain important, but they are not presented here as verified cognitive load theory claims.

That limitation is not a reason to retreat into intuition. It is a reason to use evidence with greater precision. The aim here is to build a practical discipline for teaching under uncertainty: diagnose tentatively, support narrowly, and transfer responsibility deliberately.

The false choice between structure and freedom

Discussions about teaching often become polarised around an unhelpful pair of images. In one, the teacher explains clearly, models the task and directs attention. Students are protected from confusion, but they risk becoming passive. In the other, students explore, choose and construct solutions. They exercise initiative, but some may lack the knowledge or orientation needed to make the exploration productive.

Real classrooms do not fit either image for long. A lesson is a sequence of changing relationships. At one moment, the teacher knows where attention needs to go and the student does not. At another, the student has enough orientation to make a meaningful decision. Later, a misconception becomes visible and the teacher intervenes. Then the student tries again. Good teaching is not located at one fixed point between control and freedom. It consists partly in changing the relationship at the right moments.

This is why the key unit of thought should be a transfer of responsibility, not a teaching method in isolation.

Responsibility has several parts. Who identifies the goal? Who selects a strategy? Who decides whether the current approach is working? Who notices an error? Who chooses when to ask for help? Who explains the quality criteria? Who decides whether a solution is finished? A teacher can retain responsibility for one part while transferring another. Students can choose a method within a clearly defined scientific question. They can evaluate competing explanations after the teacher has supplied essential concepts. They can plan a practical investigation while safety constraints remain non negotiable.

The central proposition of this article is therefore pedagogical, not causal: structure can support agency when it gives students better resources for consequential judgment. Structure can also reduce opportunities for agency when it takes over judgments that students could productively make themselves. The same worksheet, model answer or guiding question may help in one moment and obstruct in another.

This proposition is deliberately modest. The evidence base reviewed here does not directly establish it as a general law. It is an organising argument that must survive contact with research, classroom observation and counterexamples.

What the sequencing evidence actually says

One common version of the structure and agency debate concerns sequence. Should students receive instruction before they attempt a problem, or should they first encounter a problem that exposes the limits of their current thinking?

A meta analysis by Sinha and Kapur compared problem solving followed by instruction with instruction followed by problem solving. Across 53 studies, the synthesis found an overall advantage for problem solving followed by instruction on selected conceptual knowledge and transfer outcomes [1]. This is important evidence against the claim that instruction must always come first.

It is not evidence that problem solving should always come first.

The same review reported that the advantage was outcome specific. The procedural knowledge analysis produced a pooled estimate close to zero [1]. The reviewed claim is therefore narrower: conceptual knowledge and transfer showed an overall advantage in the synthesis, while procedural knowledge did not show the same general pattern. A nonsignificant pooled estimate does not establish that the sequences are equivalent in every classroom or for every procedural outcome.

The review also has boundaries that matter for teachers. Most comparisons concerned mathematics or basic sciences. Studies varied in population, implementation, fidelity and measurement. Candidate studies were identified through citation searching seeded by five Productive Failure papers and checked against a previous qualitative review, rather than through a broad independent search across many databases. One of the authors developed the Productive Failure approach, which does not invalidate the work but makes author allegiance a relevant source consideration. The pooled result was heterogeneous. It should not be converted into a universal rule.

A primary study by Loibl and colleagues sharpens that caution [2]. The experiment involved Dutch secondary mathematics students and compared problem solving before instruction with instruction before problem solving. It also varied whether students worked with contrasting cases. The conceptual posttest showed no statistically significant main effect of phase order. That null result does not prove equivalence. The conceptual measure had low reliability, overall scores were low, instruction consisted of a brief recorded video, and the study covered one mathematics topic in two academically oriented Dutch school tracks.

The procedural result pointed in another direction. On an immediate two item procedural fluency measure, students who received instruction before problem solving scored higher than students who solved problems before instruction [2]. This was one bounded experiment, with one topic, a short instructional treatment and an immediate outcome. It does not overturn the meta analysis. It shows why the outcome, timing, population and implementation must remain attached to every sequence claim.

Two further studies by Nachtigall and colleagues examined productive failure beyond STEM domains [3]. Both took place in a university out of school laboratory with German tenth grade classes studying social science research methods. Whole classes, rather than individual students, were assigned to conditions. Both groups eventually received explanation. The difference concerned whether students attempted the problem before that explanation or received explanation first and then practised.

The first study showed a negative pattern for the problem first condition on the measured learning outcomes. The second showed a null pattern. They should not be collapsed into two harmful effects. Nor do they show that explanation was absent from productive failure. The defensible synthesis across the reviewed sources is this:

Across these sources, placing problem solving before instruction did not uniformly improve the measured learning outcomes.

That sentence is less dramatic than either slogan in the debate. It is also more useful. It directs the teacher towards four questions:

  1. What kind of learning outcome matters here?
  2. What knowledge and strategies do students bring to the task?
  3. What happens after the initial attempt?
  4. What will the teacher do with the information revealed by students' attempts?

The phrase productive struggle can otherwise hide several different events. A student may compare ideas, notice a contradiction and reorganise a model. A student may also repeat an unproductive strategy, wait for rescue, or become occupied with avoiding embarrassment. The presence of difficulty does not tell us which event is occurring. The reviewed sources do not show that placing problem solving before later instruction will by itself create a valuable intellectual process.

This leads to a practical distinction. A problem can come before a full explanation without coming before all orientation. The teacher may establish the question, define essential terms, show how materials are used, make the success criteria visible, and provide a way to record partial ideas. Students can then attempt, compare and revise. The relevant choice is not simply explanation or no explanation. It is which responsibilities are held by the teacher now, which are given to students now, and what information the next phase should use.

Motivation changes the meaning of support

Sequence evidence tells only part of the story. A student can complete a well structured task without experiencing ownership. Another can welcome the same structure because it makes meaningful participation possible. To understand the difference, teachers need a motivational lens as well as an instructional one.

Bureau and colleagues synthesised research on antecedents of autonomous and controlled forms of motivation [4]. In their bivariate meta analyses, measured teacher autonomy support was positively associated with intrinsic motivation and identified motivation, and negatively associated with amotivation. These were reliability corrected correlations across varied educational levels, countries, reporters, measures and contexts. They were not causal effects.

The distinction matters. The synthesis does not show that a particular autonomy supportive move will cause a particular student to become intrinsically motivated. Heterogeneity was very high. Publication bias analyses suggested that the unadjusted associations for intrinsic and identified motivation may have been overstated.

Even with those limitations, the pattern supplies a useful boundary. Motivation cannot be treated as a decorative extra added after the instructional design is complete. How students experience purpose, choice and ownership is related to the quality of their motivation. A teacher who wants students to accept increasing responsibility must pay attention to this relationship.

Autonomy support is not the same thing as leaving students alone. In this article, the term is used cautiously to name practices that make room for meaningful student perspective and volition. It does not imply unlimited choice. It does not remove curricular aims, safety rules, deadlines or quality criteria. A choice between two equally meaningless tasks does not create agency. Neither does asking students to select a method when they cannot yet understand the consequences of that selection.

The practical question is whether a choice carries intelligible consequences. Students may choose which evidence to present, which model to test, which misconception to investigate, or which representation best communicates an explanation. The teacher can preserve the learning goal while varying the route. In other moments, the route itself is the object of learning and should not be chosen too quickly. A student learning to design a controlled investigation needs eventually to decide which variables matter. If the teacher always supplies the design, the student can complete the practical work without learning to make that judgment.

Agency therefore requires more than activity. It involves authorship of consequential decisions. It also requires the ability to revise those decisions in response to evidence and criteria.

Four modes, not four kinds of student

Passenger, Achiever, Resister and Explorer can help teachers notice different relationships to responsibility. They become dangerous when they are treated as explanations.

The terms in this article are not a validated psychological typology. They do not describe stable traits. No pupil should be recorded, grouped or discussed as if one of the labels names who they are. The modes are prompts for teacher inquiry. Each should end with a question mark.

Passenger?

In Passenger mode, a student appears to wait for direction, accept the route chosen by others, or complete only the parts that have been made explicit. The visible pattern may be passivity. Its cause is unknown.

The student may not understand the goal. The task may require language that is not yet accessible. The student may be processing slowly, protecting themselves from public error, waiting because previous lessons rewarded waiting, or simply tired. They may know that the teacher will eventually narrow the task. What looks like low agency can therefore be a rational response to the classroom system.

The wrong response is to diagnose the label and prescribe freedom. Removing support can turn waiting into failure without revealing why the waiting occurred. The teacher needs a small probe.

One option is to retain the goal and reduce only the first decision. Instead of asking, “What do you want to do?”, ask, “Which of these two observations should we explain first, and why?” Another option is to ask for a prediction before supplying the next step. A third is to make the success criterion visible and invite the student to identify which part of their work already meets it.

These are not treatments for a Passenger type. They are ways to test whether a modest, intelligible responsibility changes participation. If it does, the teacher has learned something useful about the present task. If it does not, the result is not a diagnosis. A different barrier may be operating.

Achiever?

In Achiever mode, a student appears strongly oriented towards successful performance. They may work hard, meet criteria and seek efficient confirmation. This can be academically productive. It can also narrow the range of risks the student is willing to take.

The teacher's challenge is not to reduce achievement. It is to widen the meaning of success. If every task has one visible route and rapid completion receives the strongest recognition, a capable student can become highly skilled at reading the teacher rather than investigating the subject.

A useful probe is to preserve standards while introducing a justified choice that cannot be solved by speed alone. In science, students might compare two plausible models and state what evidence would discriminate between them. In a written explanation, they might identify the strongest counterargument to their own account. The success criterion then includes revision, not only correctness.

Achiever mode can also appear when students ask repeatedly, “Is this enough?” That question may reflect sensible quality monitoring. It may also indicate that responsibility for judgment has remained with the teacher. Instead of answering only yes or no, the teacher can return the work to shared criteria: “Which criterion are you least certain about, and what in your answer makes you uncertain?”

The shift is small. The teacher does not hide the standard. The student begins to use it.

Resister?

In Resister mode, the student challenges the task, avoids participation, disputes the teacher's framing, or protects control by refusing the offered route. Resistance can be inconvenient. It can also contain information.

The information is not automatically correct. A student can resist a worthwhile demand. Nor should every curricular decision become a negotiation. The teacher remains responsible for safety, inclusion, shared time and legitimate educational aims.

Still, resistance may signal that the purpose is opaque, the route feels controlling, the public risk is too high, or the task offers no credible way to act with competence. A purely disciplinary response may secure compliance while leaving the relationship to learning unchanged.

A small probe can separate purpose from route. The teacher might say, “The goal is fixed: explain why the temperature changed. You may choose whether to use the particle diagram or the measurement table first.” Another probe is to ask the student to identify the part of the task that seems least justified. The response can reveal whether the issue concerns difficulty, relevance, exposure or control.

None of this means that resistance should be romanticised. Refusal does not prove hidden insight. The teacher is gathering information while maintaining the learning boundary. The aim is to make a path back into responsible participation possible.

Explorer?

In Explorer mode, a student generates possibilities, tests ideas, follows questions and tolerates some uncertainty. This mode is often treated as the ideal. It also needs structure.

Exploration can drift. A student can produce many ideas without comparing them, change variables without recording what changed, or pursue an interesting question that no longer serves the lesson. Agency without criteria can become motion without learning.

The teacher's task is not to pull the student back into compliance. It is to strengthen the quality of exploration. Ask what evidence would count against the current idea. Ask the student to name the decision they are making. Ask them to record one abandoned explanation and why it was abandoned. Set a checkpoint at which the exploration must be communicated to someone else.

An Explorer may need fewer prompts about what to do and more prompts about how to judge. The responsibility transfer is therefore different from the one offered to a Passenger. This is precisely why the modes cannot be fixed categories. The appropriate support depends on the present relationship between the student, the task and the available criteria.

Diagnose tentatively

Teachers must make decisions before they possess complete explanations. Waiting for certainty is not an option. The solution is not to stop diagnosing, but to diagnose tentatively.

A tentative diagnosis has three features.

First, it stays close to observation. “She has not started after three minutes and has reread the first instruction twice” is more useful than “She is unmotivated.” “He selected the familiar procedure without comparing alternatives” is more useful than “He is an Achiever.” Observation preserves possibilities that labels close.

Second, it generates more than one plausible explanation. A student who is silent may be disengaged, uncertain, processing, translating, monitoring the social situation, or preparing a response. An accessibility barrier may resemble disengagement. Processing latency may resemble disengagement. Public verbal initiative is not the only acceptable expression of agency.

These are pedagogical safeguards, not claims that the present evidence package has established a cross field causal model. Their purpose is ethical and practical. A teacher should avoid converting ambiguous behaviour into a stable account of character.

Third, a tentative diagnosis leads to the smallest useful change. Change one feature of support when possible. Clarify the purpose but keep the task. Reduce public exposure but keep the reasoning demand. Provide a worked starting point but leave the comparison open. Offer two representations but retain the same criterion.

The response to that change is information, not proof. If participation improves after the purpose is clarified, lack of purpose may have contributed. The change does not prove that purpose was the only cause. It certainly does not establish a pupil's readiness level or mode.

This distinction protects against a seductive classroom error: treating every successful intervention as confirmation of the story that motivated it. A student may respond for reasons the teacher did not anticipate. The probe should improve the next decision without pretending to reveal the whole learner.

Support narrowly

Support is easiest to remove when it has a clearly defined job. Broad support tends to become the environment. Narrow support can be inspected, adjusted and transferred.

Consider a science task in which students must explain why a sealed syringe becomes harder to compress. The teacher could provide the question, the equipment, a safety boundary, a particle representation and a complete explanation template. Students might produce correct work. It would be difficult to know which judgments they could make independently.

A narrower design assigns different jobs to different supports.

  • The equipment boundary protects safety.
  • A short demonstration ensures that everyone can operate the syringe.
  • A diagram makes an invisible model available for discussion.
  • A comparison question directs attention towards the relationship between volume and particle spacing.
  • A criterion states that the explanation must connect observation to model.

The teacher can then decide which element should be transferred. Perhaps students choose the most informative observation. Perhaps they create the diagram. Perhaps they compare two explanations, one of which confuses particle size with spacing. The task remains structured, but student judgment has a visible place.

This also makes fading more honest, even though this article makes no general research claim about a universal fading sequence. A teacher can say what is being removed and why. “Yesterday I supplied the comparison table. Today your group will decide which measurements belong in it.” “I will still give you the safety constraints, but you will design the order of trials.” “The criterion remains the same, but I will not mark the point where your explanation becomes inconsistent.”

Narrow support helps students notice the changing contract. It helps the teacher notice whether responsibility has actually moved.

Transfer responsibility deliberately

Responsibility does not transfer merely because the teacher becomes quiet. Silence can leave the original authority structure intact. Students may continue to guess what the teacher wants, only with less information.

A deliberate transfer names the decision, provides the resources needed to make it, and creates a later occasion for evaluation.

For example:

“You will decide which model best explains the evidence. I will make sure the three models are understood. Your group must record one reason for rejecting each model. At the checkpoint, you may revise your choice.”

The teacher still structures the intellectual space. Students own a consequential judgment within it.

This approach avoids two weak versions of choice. The first is cosmetic choice, such as selecting a font, colour or order of presentation when those decisions have little bearing on the learning. The second is premature strategic choice, where students are asked to select a method before they can understand the method's consequences.

Meaningful agency is not measured by the number of choices. One serious judgment can matter more than ten decorative options.

Deliberate transfer also requires return. Students need to see what happened because of their choice. A prediction is compared with data. A model is revised. A plan is evaluated against criteria. Without return, choice can become expression without learning.

The return phase is also where the teacher's expertise becomes visible without cancelling student authorship. The teacher can name a pattern, introduce a concept or demonstrate a more powerful representation after students have produced material that gives the explanation a purpose. The sequence evidence reviewed earlier suggests that such problem first designs can benefit selected conceptual and transfer outcomes under some conditions [1]. The same evidence also warns that they do not uniformly improve every outcome [1][2][3].

The question is not whether students discovered the canonical answer unaided. It is whether their initial work created useful contrasts, questions or commitments that the next phase could develop.

A practical cycle for classroom use

The following cycle is a design heuristic, not a validated algorithm.

1. Name the responsibility

Before the lesson, identify one judgment that students should increasingly own. Examples include selecting relevant evidence, choosing a representation, planning a comparison, deciding whether an explanation meets criteria, or recognising when help is needed.

Avoid goals such as “students work independently.” Independence is too broad to guide support. Name what the student will decide.

2. Define the non negotiable boundary

Decide what the teacher must retain for safety, inclusion, curriculum, time or access. In a chemistry practical, handling rules may be fixed. In a discussion, the requirement to justify claims with evidence may be fixed. In an assessment preparation lesson, the relevant curriculum content may be fixed.

Clear boundaries can reduce avoidable uncertainty. They can also make the remaining space for agency more credible.

3. Observe without typing

Record what the student does in relation to the named responsibility. Do they wait, choose rapidly, reject the task, generate alternatives, seek repeated confirmation, or revise after evidence?

Use Passenger, Achiever, Resister and Explorer only as private question prompts. Never let the label replace the observation.

4. Generate competing explanations

List at least two plausible reasons for the observed pattern. Include an access explanation when relevant. Include a purpose explanation. Include the possibility that the task design, not the student, is producing the pattern.

5. Change one support feature

Make the smallest change likely to distinguish among the explanations or improve participation. Clarify purpose. Reduce the first decision. Provide a representation. Make the criterion visible. Lower public exposure. Add a comparison. Delay an answer. Bring a checkpoint forward.

Changing one feature is a heuristic. The response cannot identify cause with certainty.

6. Return the judgment

Ask the student to use the resource. Do not let support complete the judgment for them. If the criterion is made visible, the student applies it. If two examples are provided, the student compares them. If the first step is supplied, the student decides the second.

7. Review the transfer

At the end of the phase, ask what the student decided, what changed and what support is still doing useful work. The teacher can then retain, modify or remove that support in the next cycle.

This cycle is intentionally slower than immediate labelling and faster than waiting for a complete psychological explanation. It is a way to act responsibly under uncertainty.

What this looks like in lower secondary science

Science offers many opportunities for structured agency because explanations must answer to observations, models and criteria.

Example 1: density and competing explanations

Students observe that a small metal object sinks while a larger wooden object floats. The teacher wants them to move beyond size as the explanation.

A fully directed route would provide the density formula, measurements and conclusion. A minimally directed route would ask students to investigate freely. A responsibility transfer design can separate the jobs.

The teacher fixes the question, provides safe materials and ensures that mass and volume can be measured. Students decide which pair of objects offers the strongest test of the “larger objects sink” idea. They must state what result would weaken that idea. After the trial, the teacher introduces or consolidates density as the more powerful relation.

A Passenger may need a choice between two informative object pairs. An Achiever may need to justify why a chosen pair is diagnostic rather than merely complete the calculation. A Resister may need the fixed purpose distinguished from the selectable route. An Explorer may need a checkpoint requiring a clear comparison before adding more objects.

These are mode hypotheses, not pupil descriptions.

Example 2: the particle model

Students explain a change of state using particles. Some reproduce memorised phrases without connecting them to the observation. Others draw elaborate diagrams that do not conserve the number of particles.

The teacher can make one criterion explicit: the explanation must connect a visible change to a change in particle movement or arrangement without inventing or deleting matter. Students then compare two diagrams and decide which better meets the criterion.

Instruction can come before or after the comparison depending on the purpose. If the representation conventions are unfamiliar, the teacher may first model how particles are shown. If students already use the conventions, an initial comparison may expose a productive contrast. The reviewed sequence evidence gives no universal answer. It tells us to attach the sequence to outcome, prior task resources and what the follow up instruction will do [1][2][3].

Example 3: planning an investigation

Students investigate how temperature affects the time needed for a tablet to dissolve. Safety and available equipment are fixed. The teacher wants students to learn about fair comparisons.

At first, the teacher may supply the independent variable and ask students to decide what must be kept constant. Later, students may choose the range of temperatures and justify it. In another cycle, they may decide how many repeated trials are needed within the available time.

The point is not to remove structure in equal steps. It is to transfer a named methodological judgment and then evaluate it. A student who can follow a supplied method has demonstrated something valuable. They have not yet demonstrated that they can design the comparison.

Example 4: writing a scientific explanation

Students answer: “Why did the temperature stop rising while the ice was melting?”

The teacher can provide a three part structure: observation, model, connection. That structure can function as access support. It becomes anti agentic only if it supplies every intellectual decision indefinitely. Students might first select the relevant observation, then choose between two model statements, and finally write the connection in their own words. Later, they can evaluate whether the three part structure is still useful.

The same visible silence during this task might arise from different causes. One student is searching for the right scientific term. Another does not understand the phenomenon. A third knows the answer but avoids public risk. A fourth is waiting for the teacher to supply the sentence. The mode lens should multiply hypotheses before it narrows action.

Inclusion requires diagnostic humility

Agency is often recognised through visible initiative: speaking first, moving quickly, proposing an idea in public or working without requests for clarification. This creates an equity risk.

Public verbal initiative is not the only plausible expression of agency. A student may exercise agency by choosing to observe before speaking, revising privately, requesting a representation, using another linguistic route, or declining a public performance while still making an intellectual decision.

This article's evidence set does not establish specific claims about multilingual science reasoning, disability or processing speed. Those candidate claims were removed because the relevant sources did not pass the final access and review gate. The safeguards remain as normative commitments and pedagogical hypotheses, not research findings.

That distinction should be visible in practice. A teacher can ask whether an accessibility barrier may resemble disengagement without asserting that it does in a particular case. A teacher can allow additional processing time without diagnosing a processing condition. A teacher can offer a diagram or written response route without lowering the conceptual criterion.

Diagnostic humility does not mean low expectations. It means separating the demand from one narrow way of displaying readiness. The student still engages with evidence, explanation and revision. The route into that engagement may differ.

The four modes should therefore never be used to create stable support groups. A group called Passengers could risk reinforcing the behaviour it was intended merely to observe. The lens is most useful at the level of a moment, task or decision.

The bounded role of AI

Generative AI can easily intensify the central problem of this article. It can supply explanations, plans, feedback and language so quickly that a learner appears productive while important judgments have moved out of view. It can also provide alternative representations or low exposure opportunities for rehearsal.

This research package establishes no causal claim about generative AI in Swedish lower secondary teaching. The present section is therefore a design boundary, not an evidence review.

The key question is not whether AI is allowed. It is which responsibility the tool is taking.

If an AI system generates the research question, selects the evidence, writes the explanation and evaluates the result, the student's activity may be reduced to acceptance. If the student supplies a claim, asks the system for two counterarguments, checks them against sources and records why one was rejected, the tool may support a consequential judgment that remains with the student.

A teacher can apply the same three disciplines used elsewhere in the article.

Diagnose tentatively. A polished AI assisted answer does not reveal which parts the student understands. Ask for a decision history, a rejected alternative or an oral explanation of one connection.

Support narrowly. Give the tool one defined job. It might clarify a term, propose a counterexample, translate a question for access, or format observations. Avoid allowing it to absorb the entire intellectual sequence.

Protect sustained reading and writing. A summary may support access, but if it becomes the only route, the student no longer practises holding an extended argument in mind. Decide which text must be read in full, what notes the student must make, and which synthesis they must write before asking AI to compress or reformulate it.

Transfer deliberately. State what the student must still decide and how that decision will be evaluated. The student might choose which generated explanation best fits the evidence and identify an unsupported sentence.

AI should not become the framework running through the whole lesson. It is one possible support whose job must be visible. When its job cannot be named, it is difficult to know what responsibility remains with the learner.

What teachers should not conclude

The reviewed evidence supports a focused set of statements. It also leaves large gaps.

It does not establish Passenger, Achiever, Resister and Explorer as valid psychological types. The modes are situational questions created for pedagogical reflection.

It does not establish a universal order of problem solving and instruction. A synthesis found benefits for selected conceptual and transfer outcomes, but not the same pattern for procedural knowledge [1]. A bounded secondary mathematics experiment found no conceptual advantage and an immediate procedural advantage for instruction first [2]. Two non STEM studies supplied negative and null counterexamples under particular conditions [3].

It does not establish that autonomy support causes motivation. The reviewed meta analysis reported associations across heterogeneous and mainly correlational evidence [4].

It does not establish that clear structure causes agency. That connection remains the organising pedagogical argument of this article, not a verified causal claim.

It does not establish a formula for adapting support to individual readiness. The S18 classroom scaffolding source could not be inspected in the present environment and was excluded. No evidence from that source enters the article.

It does not establish that visible behaviour reveals its cause. The recommendation to generate competing explanations is a safeguard for teacher judgment.

It does not establish effects of generative AI. The AI section contains bounded design questions only.

These exclusions make the article less encyclopaedic. They also make it more trustworthy. A useful teaching argument should show where research ends and professional judgment begins.

A planning page for the next lesson

Teachers can use the following questions without adopting the four labels at all.

Purpose

  • What should students understand or be able to judge by the end?
  • Why might that purpose be intelligible to them?
  • Which part of the purpose is fixed, and which part can be shaped with students?

Responsibility

  • What is one consequential decision students should make?
  • What decision will the teacher retain?
  • What evidence or criterion will allow students to review their decision?

Support

  • What job is each support doing?
  • Does any support complete the judgment students are meant to practise?
  • Which support could be reduced, delayed or made optional?

Observation

  • What did the student actually do?
  • What are at least two plausible explanations?
  • Could the task design or access route be producing the pattern?

Probe

  • What is the smallest support change worth trying?
  • What response would be informative?
  • What would remain uncertain even if the response changes?

Return

  • When will the student compare the result with evidence or criteria?
  • How will revision be made visible?
  • What responsibility should move next?

The page is intentionally built around decisions rather than personalities. It can be used for an individual, a group or the whole class. It can also reveal when the teacher has planned activity without planning agency.

Conclusion: structure should leave something to own

The best argument for structure is not that students should become compliant. It is that knowledge, language, examples, criteria and feedback can make more meaningful judgment possible. The best argument for agency is not that students should be left alone. It is that learning includes becoming able to make and revise consequential decisions.

The tension between the two cannot be solved once. It has to be managed across a lesson.

Passenger, Achiever, Resister and Explorer can help only if every label remains a question and every question remains open to revision. A student who waits may need access, purpose, a smaller first decision or a classroom that stops rewarding waiting. A high performer may need to use criteria rather than merely read the teacher. A student who resists may need a route back into meaningful control. A student who explores may need stronger standards for comparison and stopping.

The teacher's discipline is to diagnose tentatively, support narrowly and transfer responsibility deliberately.

Research gives no universal sequence. It does show that problem solving before instruction can support selected outcomes under some conditions, while other outcomes and studies show different patterns [1][2][3]. It also shows that measured teacher autonomy support is associated with more autonomous forms of motivation and less amotivation, while leaving causal questions open [4].

The rest is professional judgment, but not unstructured intuition. Teachers can name the responsibility, define the boundary, observe without typing, test a small change and return the judgment to the learner.

Good support should help the student do something they could not yet do alone. Good transfer should ensure that the student eventually has something real to own.
