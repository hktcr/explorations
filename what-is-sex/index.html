The answer depends on the question

What sex actually is in humans and across the animal kingdom, how many sexes there are, and why gamete roles are not the same thing as permanent kinds of individual

August 1, 2026≈ 41 min read64 bibliographic records checked

Ask how many sexes there are and the honest answer begins with another question: what are you counting? Across animals, eggs and sperm define two gametic roles, but those roles are distributed among bodies in strikingly different ways. Most described animal species have separate females and males. Many others are simultaneous hermaphrodites, change sex during life, or sometimes reproduce without fertilization. Humans add further questions about bodily traits, identity, and law. The apparent contradiction dissolves when the levels are kept apart.

The short answer

For humans, there are two reproductive sexes in the narrow evolutionary sense: female and male. These correspond to the two kinds of gametes involved in human sexual reproduction, large ova and small sperm. There is no third human gamete type. This definition is standard in a major strand of evolutionary biology, and it explains why the terms female and male can be applied across species whose chromosomes, anatomy, and sex determining mechanisms differ radically.[3][4]

That answer is true, but it is not the whole truth about human bodies. An individual person's sex can be described through several connected traits, including chromosomes, gonads, anatomy, hormones, and reproductive development.[9][10][11][12]

Most people can be parsimoniously classified around two common bodily patterns, while some people have congenital variations in which one or more traits do not follow either pattern straightforwardly.[6][9] The National Academies therefore recommends treating sex as a multidimensional construct rather than using one category as a proxy for every anatomical and physiological trait.[1]

Across the animal kingdom, two gamete classes do not require two permanent classes of individual. A widely cited synthesis estimates hermaphroditism in about 5 to 6 percent of animal species.[23][24] Species rich insects largely lack hermaphrodites.[24] When insects are excluded, the estimate rises to roughly one third.[23] These are approximate species weighted figures, not a census of every alternative reproductive system.

Some animals perform both reproductive roles at once, and some change from one to the other during life.[24] Some vertebrates can also produce offspring from eggs without fertilization.[42][56]

Gender adds another layer. Definitions vary among fields, but current health and social research commonly distinguishes gender, gender identity, and gender expression. The World Health Organization describes gender in terms of socially constructed norms, roles, and relations, while gender identity refers to a person's internal experience. The vocabularies people use for identity vary across cultures and over time, so science has no timeless universal catalogue with a fixed number of identities.[1][2] Legal systems add still another count. Sweden currently registers two legal sexes, woman and man, even though people may change their registered sex under the law that came into force on 1 July 2025.[20][21]

The most defensible answer is not “sex is simply binary” or “sex is simply a spectrum.” It is: reproductive roles are binary in humans, while the traits through which bodies develop and are classified are multidimensional.

One word, several questions

For this article, it helps to treat the Swedish word kön as an overloaded label rather than as one scientific variable. In this article, biological sex, gender identity, social gender, legal sex, and genital anatomy are kept as separate variables even when ordinary language groups them under one label. English separates some of these meanings with the words sex and gender, but the separation is not perfect. Historical usage differs among disciplines, institutions, and countries. A debate can therefore look like a factual disagreement even when its participants are classifying different things.[1]

Consider five questions that are often compressed into “How many sexes are there?”[1]

None of these systems is imaginary. Reproductive biology is real. Congenital variation is real. Gender identity and social treatment have measurable effects on health and life chances. Legal records have practical consequences. Confusion begins when one system is treated as if it automatically answered every question posed in another.[1][2][19]

The gamete definition

Sexual reproduction joins genetic material from different cells. In many multicellular organisms, including humans, the gametes differ greatly in size. This is called anisogamy. The larger, resource rich gamete is the ovum. The smaller gamete is the sperm. In evolutionary biology, female is the sex associated with producing the larger gamete, while male is the sex associated with producing the smaller gamete.[3][14]

This definition does useful work. Chromosomes cannot define female and male across all vertebrates because the mechanisms that initiate female and male development show remarkable diversity and plasticity. Reviews document different sex determining systems, master genes, regulatory networks, and evolutionary transitions. Yet when organisms reproduce anisogamously, the names female and male still track the two gamete roles.[3][13]

The definition also explains why a third body form is not automatically a third reproductive sex. To establish a third sex in this strict sense, a species would need a third gamete class with a distinct role in reproduction, not simply a third chromosome pattern, appearance, hormone level, or identity. No such third gamete class exists in humans.[3][14][24]

Mental imageImagine two biological strategies at fertilization. One invests heavily in each of a smaller number of large cells. The other produces many small cells that compete to reach them. Around those two strategies, evolution builds gonads, ducts, hormones, anatomy, and behavior. The architecture can vary. The two gamete roles remain.

Why two gamete sizes?

The usual evolutionary explanation is disruptive selection. A gamete can gain by becoming larger and provisioning the resulting zygote, or by becoming smaller and increasing its number, mobility, and chance of encounter. An intermediate cell can lose on both fronts: it is less numerous than the smallest cells and provides less material than the largest. Models differ in their details, and anisogamy evolved independently in many lineages, but this tradeoff helps explain why stable systems tend to separate toward two size strategies rather than three or a smooth continuum.[14][24][57]

Anisogamy does not mechanically determine every later difference between the sexes. A recent model found greater male investment in mating competition at high gamete density. At intermediate density, either females or males could invest more. The result depended on gamete limitation and on the competition trait being modeled.[41]

This is an explanation of an evolutionary pattern, not a claim that every egg is identical or every sperm is identical. Gametes vary within each class. The distinction concerns two contrasting reproductive strategies and their relative sizes within a species, not a universal measurement threshold shared by elephants, worms, and corals.[14][24][57]

Recent scholarship shows that even this apparently simple statement has a boundary. Some authors argue that gamete type supplies the fundamental definition of sex and gives exactly two sexes.[3][4] Others argue that it defines reproductive roles but cannot, by itself, settle every legitimate use of sex in medicine, developmental biology, or human classification.[5][6][7] The disagreement is partly empirical, but also conceptual: must one definition govern every scientific context?

Infertility is not a third sex

Consider babies, postmenopausal women, infertile adults, and people with differences of sex development. If sex is defined through gametes, do people who do not produce them have no sex?[3]

Biologists who use the gametic definition generally classify organisms by their developmental organization toward one of the reproductive roles, not by whether a particular person is fertile at a particular moment. A person can be female without currently producing ova, just as a person can have eyes while being blind. Loss or absence of function does not necessarily erase the kind of organ system that developed.[3][9]

This reasoning is strongest when the developmental pathway is clear. It becomes less straightforward in rare cases where traits are discordant, gonads are mixed or absent, or development does not resolve toward one typical pathway. Here, an individual classification may require context and clinical detail. That difficulty does not create a new gamete type. It does show why a population level definition and an individual medical description are not interchangeable.[3][9]

Hermaphroditism provides another test. Many plants and animals can perform both reproductive roles, simultaneously or at different times. A hermaphroditic individual is not necessarily a third sex. It may be one organism capable of functioning as both female and male.[23][25] In humans, the outdated and stigmatizing term “hermaphrodite” should not be used for people with intersex traits. The relevant clinical terms are intersex variations or differences of sex development, depending on the person and context.[9]

How a body becomes sexed

The slogan “XX is female and XY is male” is a useful school level shorthand, not a complete mechanism. Human sex development is a sequence of interacting processes. Chromosomal material influences gene activity. Gene networks contribute to whether undifferentiated embryonic gonadal tissue develops along testicular or ovarian pathways. Gonads produce hormones. Enzymes convert those hormones. Receptors allow tissues to respond. Internal ducts and external genitalia develop under those signals. Puberty later changes many secondary traits.[9][10][11][12]

A difference at any step can change what follows. Complete androgen insensitivity syndrome offers a clear example. A person may have a 46,XY karyotype and testes that produce androgens, while cells cannot respond normally because of variation in the androgen receptor. External genitalia can be typically female. This shows that a 46,XY karyotype does not by itself determine external anatomy, because androgen action depends on receptor function.[10]

Partial androgen insensitivity is more variable. In a clinical cohort, androgen receptor sequencing found mutations in 27 of 111 tested patients. External phenotype varied widely and was rarely predicted by receptor genotype alone.[47]

The converse kind of mismatch can also occur. In 46,XX testicular differences of sex development, a person has two X chromosomes but develops testes and typically a male phenotype. In many cases, a small fragment containing the SRY gene has moved from a Y chromosome during sperm formation. Again, the causal lesson is not that genes do not matter. It is that one chromosome label is only a proxy for a network of genes, signals, tissues, and developmental timing.[12]

Congenital adrenal hyperplasia provides a different route. In common severe forms, altered steroid synthesis can expose a 46,XX fetus to elevated androgens and produce varying degrees of masculinization of the external genitalia. Some forms can also cause dangerous salt loss and require prompt medical care. A biological variation can therefore be both part of human diversity and clinically important. Respectful language should never become an excuse to ignore a health risk.[11]

A large clinical genetics study analyzed families affected by 21 hydroxylase deficiency. Among 1,507 affected families, it compared salt wasting, simple virilizing, and nonclassical forms and found that genotype predicted phenotype for some combinations but not others.[48]

Mental imageDo not picture sex development as a single switch marked XX or XY. Picture a branching river. Chromosomes shape the headwaters, but genes, gonads, enzymes, hormones, receptors, anatomy, and puberty form successive channels. Most rivers reach one of two broad deltas. A minority follow less common channels.
Sex development is not one switch. It is a causal chain in which chromosomes, genes, gonads, hormones, receptors, anatomy, and life history usually align, but need not be interchangeable.

Intersex variations

Intersex is an umbrella term for congenital variations in sex characteristics that do not all fit typical definitions of female or male bodies. Differences of sex development, often shortened to DSD, is common in clinical literature. Some people prefer intersex, some prefer DSD, and some prefer the name of a specific diagnosis. Terminology has a history of stigma, so the person's own preference matters.[1][9]

Variations can involve chromosomes, gonads, hormone synthesis, hormone response, internal anatomy, external genitalia, or combinations of traits. They do not all have the same medical implications or lived experience. Turner syndrome, Klinefelter syndrome, androgen insensitivity, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, ovotesticular DSD, and many other conditions differ profoundly. For this article, treating them as one third biological sex would obscure this diagnostic heterogeneity.[9][10][11][12]

How common are intersex traits? The answer depends heavily on the definition. A widely repeated estimate is about 1.7 percent, but it groups a broad set of variations, including some that do not produce ambiguous genitalia and may never challenge ordinary sex assignment. A much narrower estimate focused on conditions involving discordance between chromosomal sex and phenotypic sex has been reported at roughly 0.018 percent.[15][16] There is no honest single prevalence figure without stating what was counted.

This numerical dispute teaches a general lesson. Categories do not merely discover totals. Their inclusion rules help produce the total. The contrast between the estimates illustrates how changing the inclusion definition changes the reported prevalence. For this article, no prevalence figure is interpreted without its inclusion criteria.[15][16]

Intersex variations neither prove that reproductive roles are unlimited nor disappear because human reproduction uses two gametes. They show that the mapping from reproductive roles to whole individual bodies is not perfectly one to one. Any account that denies either part is incomplete.[1][5][6][9]

Binary, bimodal, or spectrum?

Three words dominate public discussion: binary, bimodal, and spectrum. Each can be useful. Each can also mislead.[1][5][6]

Binary

Here, binary means two possible categories. Human gamete type is binary: ova or sperm. Reproductive role is therefore binary in this narrow framework.[3][4][6]

Chromosomes are not simply two values, because aneuploid and mosaic karyotypes occur. Hormone concentrations are quantitative. Genital anatomy and secondary traits vary. Calling “all aspects of sex” binary silently changes a precise claim into a much broader one.[5][6][9][10][11][12]

Bimodal

A distribution is bimodal when observations cluster around two common peaks. Bimodal does not mean that every value lies at one of two points. It means that two modes dominate the particular distribution being measured.[31] In the current debate, one peer reviewed account describes two main bodily feature clusters with overlap and fuzzy boundaries, while epidemiological guidance warns against using any one measure as a proxy for every chromosome, hormone, organ, and secondary characteristic.[6][8]

Spectrum

Spectrum language correctly emphasizes variation and can be humane and useful in discussing multidimensional sex traits. Yet it can suggest that every trait varies along one common line from female to male. Biology is more complicated. Bodily characteristics may form two main clusters with overlap and fuzzy boundaries without every case being represented faithfully by a single point on a ruler. For research, measuring several relevant dimensions is often more informative than collapsing them into one category.[1][5][6][8]

This is why the National Academies and several epidemiologists recommend measuring the specific dimensions relevant to the research question rather than asking one vague sex or gender item to stand in for all of them.[1][8]

A careful formulation: Human reproductive sex is binary at the level of gamete class. Human sex traits are multidimensional, strongly clustered into two common patterns, and not universally concordant. “Binary” and “spectrum” are therefore answers to different models, not magic words that settle every question.

Why experts still disagree

It may seem surprising that biologists, physicians, philosophers, and social scientists can inspect much of the same evidence and still disagree about whether sex is binary. The disagreement does not usually concern the existence of ova and sperm, or the existence of intersex variations. It concerns what a scientific category is supposed to do.[3][4][5][6][7]

One approach seeks a definition that is stable across the tree of life. Gamete type performs well. It lets an evolutionary biologist compare a flowering plant, a fish, and a mammal without pretending that all three use human chromosomes or anatomy. It also connects the categories female and male to a powerful theory of anisogamy, sexual selection, parental investment, and reproductive conflict. From this perspective, chromosomes and bodies are mechanisms and manifestations of sex, while gamete role is sex itself.[3][4]

A second approach begins with human development and medicine. Here, a sex classification may be intended to communicate something about chromosomes, hormones, secondary characteristics, disease presentation, drug response, or treatment effectiveness. Gamete role may not be the mechanism under investigation. Researchers in this tradition argue that sex is better understood as several partly connected variables.[5][8]

A third approach asks how categories are made and used. A definition can be biologically coherent yet still fail to justify a legal rule. Conversely, an administrative category can be useful without constituting a newly discovered fact of reproductive biology. Scholars working in philosophy, ethics, law, or science studies therefore examine not only whether a classification tracks nature, but why that feature was selected, whose needs the classification serves, and what consequences follow.[6][7]

These approaches overlap, but they optimize for different virtues:[3][5][8]

ApproachMain goalStrengthRisk if used alone
EvolutionaryExplain reproductive roles across speciesStable, causal definition grounded in anisogamyMay be too coarse for individual clinical questions

Scientific categories are tools. This does not mean that anything goes. A definition must be explicit, internally coherent, supported by evidence, and suitable for its purpose. It also must not slide unnoticed from one purpose to another.[1][8]

The phrase “there is no consensus” requires care as well. The sources compared here repeatedly recognize two gamete classes in anisogamous reproduction and variation among human sex traits. They disagree over whether gamete role alone should govern every scientific and social use of the word sex. That narrower but important disagreement is visible in peer reviewed exchanges published as recently as 2025 and 2026.[3][4][5][6][7]

Political debate often erases this structure. One side may cite anisogamy as if it answered how a hospital should record every relevant trait. Another may cite developmental variation as if it created additional gamete classes. Both moves take a valid observation out of the scale where it does its best work.[1][3][5][6]

A census of the animal kingdom

Humans are a narrow sample of animal reproductive diversity. Across the animal kingdom, the same two gamete roles are distributed among bodies in several ways.[23][24]

The technical term for a species with separate female and male individuals is gonochoric. Comparative syntheses describe gonochorism as the majority condition and estimate hermaphroditism in roughly 5 to 6 percent of animal species.[23][24] This is a species weighted estimate from an incomplete and taxonomically uneven record, not a direct measurement of every animal species or every alternative reproductive system. It supports a broad conclusion: separate female and male individuals dominate the described species count.

Classification rules still matter. In a separate macroevolutionary analysis, a species was coded as gonochoric if it had separate sexes at any point in its sexually active life cycle. Sequential hermaphrodites were therefore included as gonochoric because each individual performs one role during a given adult phase.[23] Other zoological accounts group those same species with hermaphrodites because one individual performs both roles over a lifetime. This coding rule illustrates how definitions alter a reconstruction, but it did not generate the separate global estimate of 5 to 6 percent hermaphroditism.

But the headline percentage hides a huge sampling effect. Insects make up an enormous share of described animal species, and hermaphroditism is largely absent from this species rich group.[24]

Remove insects from the calculation and hermaphroditism is estimated to occur in about one third of animal species.[23][24] In other words, the answer depends not only on what we count, but also on whether every species, every lineage, or every individual receives equal weight.

Common among species, widespread among lineages

Species frequency is not the same as evolutionary breadth. Hermaphroditism may account for only about one species in twenty when insects are included, yet a broad reconstruction reports at least one hermaphroditic species in more than 70 percent of animal phyla. The exact denominator changes with taxonomy, but the pattern has appeared in distant branches of the animal tree rather than arising as one isolated curiosity.[23] Counting species makes insects numerically dominant. Counting major lineages makes the repeated reach of hermaphroditism much more visible.

Nor can the present day majority reveal the original animal condition. Reconstructions indicate repeated transitions between gonochorism and hermaphroditism, with some analyses finding more transitions toward hermaphroditism than away from it. The sexual system of the last common ancestor of living animals remains unresolved because different models and branch length assumptions give different answers.[23] Three questions must therefore be kept separate: which system contains the most living species, how often systems have changed, and which state was ancestral.

How do researchers recognize functional hermaphroditism?

Anatomy alone can mislead. A diagnostic review of teleost fishes emphasized that gonad structure does not always reflect reproductive function. Using conservative criteria, improved knowledge of gonadal development, and a reexamination of the primary literature, it confirmed functional hermaphroditism in 27 teleost families across seven orders.[39] This stricter standard is one reason older species lists are revised rather than merely enlarged.

Even so, the broad pattern is robust. Hermaphroditism is estimated at about 5 percent across animal species, largely because species rich insects lack hermaphrodites, but it is common in many other animal taxa, including fish and many invertebrates.[24]

Two gametic roles are common across animals. Two separate, fixed kinds of individual are also very common, but they are not a universal requirement of animal sexual reproduction.
Mental imagePicture a world map of languages. One language may have most of the speakers while minority languages occur in nearly every region. In the same way, separate sexes dominate the species count, while hermaphroditism remains scattered across most major branches of the animal tree.

One body, both roles

A simultaneous hermaphrodite can produce both eggs and sperm during the same phase of life. Many mate with partners, and some self fertilize.[24]

Hermaphroditism and selfing are not synonyms. A comparative review found that self fertilization is distributed unevenly among hermaphroditic animals. It identified reproductive assurance as one possible ecological contribution and warned that inbreeding depression and measurement biases complicate the estimates.[25]

A separate comparative study of 1,153 marine invertebrate species across three phyla found hermaphroditism more likely among species with internal fertilization, restricted offspring dispersal, or small body size. It also found that hermaphroditism became less likely at higher latitudes.[26]

No single variable explains every lineage. A review of sex allocation research across nine animal phyla found qualitative support for some predictions, including shifts associated with the number of mates, while several assumptions and predictions remained poorly supported.[34] Hermaphroditism is a family of evolutionary solutions, not one adaptation with one cause.

More forms do not mean more sexes

Animals of the same sex can look and behave radically differently. A field study of side blotched lizards documented three alternative male strategies maintained by frequency dependent selection. All were male strategies within one reproductive sex, not additional gamete defined sexes.[53]

Social insects make the distinction even sharper. In most species, queens and workers arise from a common genome but follow caste specific developmental paths. A review found the proposed role of DNA methylation in those paths to be equivocal.[40]

In honeybees, fertile queens and sterile workers are alternative adult female forms that develop from genetically identical larvae after different feeding. An experiment that altered DNA methylation machinery shifted many treated larvae toward queen development.[51] These are developmental castes among females, not additional gamete classes.

Two sexes do not mean fifty fifty

The number of sexes says nothing by itself about their numerical balance. Sex ratio research measures the proportion of offspring that are male and studies variation at the level of species, populations, individuals, and broods. One reviewed example found Seychelles warblers producing strongly female biased broods on high quality territories and strongly male biased broods on low quality territories.[33]

An operational sex ratio counts only the males and females that are sexually active or receptive at a given time. It can therefore differ sharply from a ratio of all adult males to females. A drone study of loggerhead sea turtles documented seasonal changes in this mating pool and found that the ratio of receptive females to breeding males remained near one to one during much of the period before nesting, even while the total numbers of adult females and males were strongly imbalanced.[63]

In a controlled study of sex-role-reversed broad-nosed pipefish, breeding groups had either a male biased or female biased operational sex ratio at one of two population densities. Female crowding rose as fewer males were available for mating, a pattern consistent with stronger competition among females in that system.[64]

A balanced expectation also depends on assumptions. Hamilton summarized Fisher's argument this way: if male births are less common, newborn males have better expected mating prospects, so selection favors parents that produce males. The advantage declines as the ratio approaches one to one, and the same reasoning works in reverse when females are rarer.[52] An equilibrium prediction is not a rule that every observed group must contain equal numbers.

In simultaneous hermaphrodites, counting females and males may not even be the relevant problem. Sex allocation theory instead asks how an individual divides reproductive investment between male and female function. A review found allocation becoming more female biased when the number of mates was limited and more male biased as the number of mates increased. It also found that several assumptions and predictions, including the proposed constraint from brooding, remained poorly supported.[34] Thus “How many sexes exist?”, “How many individuals of each sex are present?”, and “How much is invested in each sex function?” are three different questions.

A direct experiment in the simultaneously hermaphroditic flatworm Macrostomum lignano found that the proportion of male investment rose as mating group size increased and then reached a plateau, as the model predicted.[59]

Changing sex

Sequential hermaphrodites perform one reproductive role and later another. Protandry means male first and female later. Protogyny means female first and male later. Some fishes can change in either direction.[27]

Why change at all? An early model proposed that sex change can raise reproductive potential when age specific fecundity differs between the male and female roles. Protandry can be favored when female fecundity rises with age, whereas protogyny can be favored when early male fecundity is depressed by factors such as territoriality, inexperience, or female mate choice. Demography and spawning habits alter the predicted benefit.[54]

Sex change is not a superficial relabeling. A review describes coordinated behavioral, anatomical, neuroendocrine, and molecular transformations during natural sex change in fishes.[27]

Primary studies show how those transformations unfold in particular species. In bluehead wrasses, socially cued female to male change involves the stress axis, repression of the aromatase gene, altered gene expression, and epigenetic reprogramming.[49] Pair experiments and gonadal observations in the goby Lubricogobius exiguus found bidirectional sex change and several functional gonadal phases.[61]

A phylogenetic study sampled 4,614 teleost fish species across several sexual systems. The analysis found gonochorism to be the likely ancestral condition and reported repeated transitions among gonochorism, sequential hermaphroditism, and simultaneous hermaphroditism.[28] This sample is not a census of all fishes, but it shows both sides of the story: fixed separate sexes dominate, while alternative systems are real, diverse, and evolutionarily recurrent.

Sex change is not a fish invention. Slipper snails in the genus Crepidula mature first as males and later become females. Several caridean shrimps are protandrous, while species of Lysmata begin with a male phase and later add female function without losing the capacity to function as male.[24][32] The details differ, but the logic is the same: a life can occupy two established reproductive roles without creating a third gamete type.

Reproduction without males

Some animals can produce offspring from eggs without fertilization. This is parthenogenesis. It is observed across various vertebrate groups but not mammals. One study notes obligate parthenogenesis in about 100 vertebrate species and 1,000 invertebrate species, while facultative cases are known in some vertebrates, including sharks and rays.[42]

Parthenogenesis does not create a third gamete. It removes the immediate need for sperm. An obligately parthenogenetic lineage may currently contain eggs but no sperm at all. Its members are conventionally called female because they produce ova or egg derived propagules homologous to those of females in their sexual relatives, not because two gamete classes are secretly present in the lineage.[35] The number of sexes represented in a population, the number of gamete classes currently produced, the system in its sexual relatives, and the number of parents required for a particular birth can therefore differ.

Parthenogenesis is not evenly distributed. A systematic database counted 765 cases of female producing parthenogenesis in haplodiploid arthropods. Frequencies ranged from 0 to 38 percent among genera, and many species contained both sexual and parthenogenetic lineages.[29]

Facultative parthenogenesis also occurs in vertebrates. Genetic analysis found it in wild copperheads and cottonmouths.[56] A separate study found annually recurring parthenogenesis in captive common smooth hound sharks and excluded long term sperm storage as the explanation.[42] These are alternative reproductive rules, not extra gamete classes.

When an asexual lineage still needs sperm

The boundary between sex and asexuality is not perfectly clean. In gynogenesis, an egg needs contact with sperm to begin development, but the sperm genome is normally excluded, so the offspring inherit their nuclear genome from the mother. In hybridogenesis, part of a hybrid genome is discarded before gamete production and restored through mating in the next generation. Kleptogenetic systems may incorporate paternal genetic material only sometimes.[35]

These systems separate four events that ordinary reproduction bundles together: mating, egg activation, genetic contribution, and parenthood. A population may contain only females of one lineage while depending on sperm from males of a related sexual species. Sperm can therefore be ecologically essential without supplying the offspring's usual nuclear inheritance. Again, the gametes are still eggs and sperm, but the rules governing what each contributes have changed.[35]

A modeling study examined the ecological consequences of sperm dependent parthenogens. These hybrids produce clonal gametes but use parental species for reproduction. Its reaction and diffusion model found that they can slow competitive exclusion between sexual species or even reverse the outcome.[43]

Whether sperm is needed, whether fertilization occurs, and whether paternal DNA is inherited are three distinct biological questions.

Many switches, two roles

Two classic experiments show how environmental information can enter sex development. In map turtles, incubation temperature determined hatchling sex both in the laboratory and in nature, and the result was not caused by different mortality of the sexes. The same experiment found no temperature effect in a soft shelled turtle.[55]

In the Atlantic silverside, sex determination is controlled by both genotype and temperature during a particular period of larval development. Sex ratios among offspring from different females are variable and respond differently to temperature.[58]

A major comparative review described this as a paradox: the upstream switches evolve rapidly and differ bewilderingly among lineages, while the downstream distinction between producing relatively large eggs and relatively small sperm is strongly conserved.[24] The existence of many sex determining mechanisms therefore does not imply many gametic sexes. It implies many evolutionary routes to two reproductive roles, plus many ways of placing those roles in one body, two bodies, or one body at different times.

Even one named species need not use a single mechanism everywhere. Geographically separated populations of the Japanese wrinkled frog Rana rugosa use both XX and XY systems and ZZ and ZW systems, with mixed populations near a contact zone.[38]

In fishes, sexual pattern itself can also vary among populations as density and mating conditions change.[30] A species label is therefore sometimes a useful summary rather than an exceptionless rule.

When another organism reaches into the switch

Sex determination is not always controlled only by an animal's own genome and physical environment. Maternally inherited Wolbachia bacteria infect many arthropods and can feminize genetic males, induce parthenogenesis, kill male embryos, or create incompatibility between sperm and eggs. These manipulations favor the transmission of bacteria through eggs.[36]

In the butterfly Eurema hecabe, two Wolbachia strains transform genetic males into functional females. An antibiotic experiment produced intermediate sexual traits when bacterial density was suppressed, indicating that feminization acts continuously during larval development.[50]

Wolbachia does not invent another gamete class. It changes which hosts develop, which survive, and whether sexual reproduction succeeds. The example reveals that a sex determining system can be an ecological interaction among genomes, rather than a sealed program located entirely inside one animal.[36][50]

Mental imageThink of animal sex as a stage play with two recurring reproductive roles, egg producer and sperm producer. Evolution keeps rewriting the casting rules. Sometimes each actor receives one role for life. Sometimes every actor can play both. Sometimes the role changes with size, temperature, or social rank. Sometimes one role temporarily performs the scene alone.

Which individual are we counting?

For a solitary vertebrate, “one animal” usually seems obvious. Modular and colonial animals make the unit itself part of the problem. A coral or siphonophore can consist of repeated modules, often called polyps or zooids, produced by budding. Researchers may describe sex at the level of a module, a colony, or the whole genetic individual. A colony containing egg producing and sperm producing modules can be functionally hermaphroditic even when each reproductive module performs only one role.[37]

Life cycles add another layer. Many cnidarians alternate a sessile polyp phase with a mobile medusa phase, and sexual differentiation can be expressed differently across those stages. Some polyps multiply by budding and later produce medusae that make gametes.[37]

Colonial structure does not imply a loose or undefined sex mechanism. A genomic study of the colonial cnidarian Hydractinia symbiolongicarpus found an XX and XY system with pseudoautosomal and nonrecombining chromosome regions.[62]

Asexual budding is also distinct from sex change. In one laboratory clone of Hydra oligactis, animals reproduced by asexual budding at 18 and 22 degrees Celsius. At 10 degrees Celsius, gamete production was induced and the animals' sexual phenotypes became observable.[60]

The deeper lesson is that every percentage presupposes a counting unit. Before calling a species gonochoric or hermaphroditic, a researcher must decide which stage and which level of biological individuality carries the reproductive functions. Different defensible units can yield different descriptions of the same living system.[37]

Beyond animals

The picture becomes stranger outside the animal kingdom. Some unicellular organisms are isogamous, meaning their fusing cells or gametes are similar in size. Under a definition based on anisogamous gamete size, male and female do not apply to a strictly isogamous system. That does not make the system sexless in every broader genetic sense. It means that compatible cells can fuse without the large egg and small sperm distinction.[17][18][44]

Mental imageThink of mating types as connector standards rather than additional egg and sperm roles. Several plugs can determine which cells are compatible even when the cells are the same size. Compatibility classes and gamete classes answer different questions.

Mating types answer a separate compatibility question: which cells, nuclei, or hyphae can fuse? Fungi can have many alleles at one or more compatibility loci, generating thousands of possible mating type combinations even when the partners are not differentiated into eggs and sperm. Some fungi fuse hyphae rather than free swimming gametes. Number of mating types and number of anisogamous sexes are therefore not rival answers to one count.[18][24][44]

A molecular study found seven mating types in the ciliate Tetrahymena thermophila. The MTA and MTB proteins form a membrane complex that recognizes the cell's own and other mating types, inhibiting or activating mating accordingly.[45]

A mathematical analysis of isogamous species modeled why mating types do not increase without limit. It noted that most observed species have fewer than ten mating types and predicted fewer types as variation in mortality increased.[46]

The ciliate Tetrahymena thermophila, for example, has seven mating types, and cells can pair with a different type.[17]

Calling these “seven sexes” uses sex to mean a compatibility class. It does not imply seven gamete sizes or seven anisogamous reproductive roles.[18] The count therefore depends on the definition used.

Sex, gender, identity, and orientation

Gender is not merely a polite synonym for sex. The World Health Organization distinguishes sex related biological characteristics from gender as socially constructed norms, roles, and relations. Gender identity is a person's internal experience of gender and may or may not correspond to assigned sex.[2]

A transgender woman is a woman whose gender identity differs from the male sex assigned at birth. A transgender man is a man whose gender identity differs from the female sex assigned at birth. Nonbinary is an umbrella term for identities that are not exclusively woman or man. These statements classify identity. They do not claim that a new human gamete has been discovered.[1]

How many genders are there? Science cannot supply a timeless integer because gender categories are partly social, cultural, linguistic, and personal. Researchers can count the response options in a particular survey, the identities reported in a particular population, or the legal markers available in a particular state. They cannot infer from those counts a final inventory for every culture and time.[1][2][19]

Gender norms and discrimination influence exposure to violence, access to health care, and health outcomes.[2]

That does not make gender unreal. Money, citizenship, language, kinship, and law are also socially organized, yet they produce observable consequences. Identity can be measured, although measurement must state what dimension it captures. The National Academies explicitly treats gender as multidimensional, linking identity, expression, and social expectations.[1]

Nor are all nonbinary terms interchangeable. Hijra, Two Spirit, and other culturally specific categories emerge from distinct histories and communities. Removing them from context and presenting them as items in a universal Western list can be another kind of simplification.[1][22]

Sexual orientation is another dimension

Sexual orientation is not a synonym for sex or gender identity. It describes patterns of sexual, romantic, or emotional attraction and may also be measured through identity and behavior. Those dimensions do not always align perfectly with one another.[19] A person can therefore have a biological sex profile, a gender identity, and a sexual orientation without any one of them supplying the value of the others.

People may use different orientation labels or no label at all. These labels concern patterns of attraction, identity, and behavior rather than additional biological sexes.[19]

Legal sex

Legal recognition of gender is a state administered process. United Nations guidance evaluates access to that process through identity, privacy, equal protection, freedom from discrimination, and freedom of expression.[22]

The available legal categories and procedures depend on the jurisdiction and date.[20][21]

As of August 2026, Sweden has two legal sexes in the population register, woman and man. Since 1 July 2025, the medical process and the legal process are separated more clearly. The ordinary route is available from age 16 when the statutory conditions are met, but an application concerning a person aged 16 or 17 is made by that person's guardian. The law also provides a separate route for some children under 16 with a congenital difference of sex development, with the child's written consent required from age 12. Socialstyrelsen states explicitly that Sweden still has only those two registered categories.[20][21]

United Nations guidance recommends that legal recognition be a simple administrative process and that it recognize nonbinary identities.[22]

For this article, legal responses to identity and privacy are kept separate from the count of gamete types. In this article, a country with only two legal markers is not treated as erasing people whose identity is nonbinary or whose sex traits are intersex.

Human rights do not depend on winning a taxonomy debate. A person does not need to constitute a third reproductive sex to deserve bodily autonomy, accurate health care, protection from discrimination, or documents that allow ordinary life. Recent scholarly disagreement is especially clear on this point: biological definitions should not be smuggled into moral and legal conclusions without a separate argument.[6][7][22]

Eight claims put to the test

“Sex is determined only by chromosomes”

Verdict: false. Chromosomes are important upstream factors, but development depends on particular genes, gene regulation, gonads, hormone synthesis, enzymes, receptors, anatomy, and timing. A 46,XY person with complete androgen insensitivity may have typically female external anatomy. A 46,XX person with SRY material can develop testes. Chromosomes are powerful evidence, not a complete description of a body.[10][12]

“There are only XX women and XY men”

Verdict: false as a universal claim. Most women are 46,XX and most men are 46,XY, but this association is not exceptionless. Variations in chromosome number, gene location, and androgen response can change the relationship between karyotype, gonads, and anatomy. A rule can be highly predictive without being universal.[9][10][12]

“Intersex is a third sex”

Verdict: usually misleading. Intersex is an umbrella over many distinct variations. People with these traits are not one homogeneous reproductive class, do not share one anatomy, and do not produce a shared third gamete.[1][4][9] Some people do identify their sex as intersex, and that identity should be respected. That personal or legal usage is different from claiming a third anisogamous reproductive role.

“Intersex proves that sex is not binary”

Verdict: definition dependent. Intersex variations do not overturn the two gamete model. They do show that chromosome, gonadal, hormonal, genital, and secondary traits are not each strictly binary and perfectly aligned in every person. Thus the claim is false if “sex” means gamete role, but it identifies a real limitation if “sex” means a complete individual trait profile.[1][3][5][6][9]

“Sex is a spectrum”

Verdict: useful but incomplete. Many traits vary quantitatively, and sex development can produce combinations outside typical patterns. Yet no single female to male line captures all dimensions. “Multidimensional, with two predominant clusters” is often more precise. Spectrum language is best treated as a corrective to rigid trait essentialism, not as a replacement for all categorical biology.[1][5][6][8]

“Gender is purely a personal feeling”

Verdict: too narrow. Gender identity includes an internal dimension, but gender also refers to social roles, norms, expression, institutions, and how others classify a person. Those processes affect material outcomes. The fact that categories are socially organized does not make their effects unreal.[1][2]

“If gender has many categories, biology must have the same number”

Verdict: false. Categories at different levels need not match one for one. Seven mating types in Tetrahymena do not imply seven anisogamous gamete roles.[17][18]

For this article, the number of gender categories and the number of gamete categories are therefore kept as separate counts. Gender vocabularies classify identity and social position. Gamete categories classify reproductive role.

“Biology settles the legal and moral question”

Verdict: false. For this article, a gamete classification is not treated as sufficient to prescribe a state's rules for legal recognition.

United Nations guidance evaluates those rules through privacy, identity, equal protection, freedom from discrimination, and freedom of expression.[22]

For this article, policy conclusions are therefore treated as requiring legal and ethical premises in addition to biological facts.

The right variable for the question

The best classification is the one that answers the actual question while collecting no more sensitive information than necessary.[1][8]

This context sensitive approach avoids two symmetrical mistakes. The first is biological reductionism, where one abstract reproductive model is assumed to answer every medical, social, and legal question. The second is biological erasure, where respect for identity is treated as a reason not to measure anatomy or physiology when they are causally relevant.[1][3][5][6][8]

Precision medicine should ask about the mechanism it needs. Sports, prisons, statistics, and clinical guidelines raise harder policy questions because they combine bodies, risk, fairness, privacy, and rights. Biology can inform those choices. It cannot make all the value judgments for us.[1][7]

A better final answer

What, then, is sex? It is both a reproductive distinction and, in individual organisms, a developmental organization expressed through multiple traits. Across anisogamous animals, eggs and sperm define two reproductive roles. Nature then distributes those roles in several ways: between fixed females and males, within simultaneous hermaphrodites, across successive phases of a sex changing life, or alongside episodes of reproduction without fertilization.[1][3][5][6][14]

How common are two sexes? If this means two gamete classes, they are the defining pattern of anisogamous animal reproduction. If it means separate female and male individuals, comparative work identifies that as the dominant arrangement among described animal species. A widely cited estimate places hermaphroditism at roughly 5 to 6 percent overall and nearly one third when insects are excluded. Those estimates do not classify every asexual or mixed system and should not be converted into a precise complementary percentage. A separate phylogenetic study illustrates how coding sequential sex changers can alter a reconstruction. Two roles are more universal than two fixed kinds of body.[23][24]

For humans, sex traits usually cohere around two common reproductive pathways, but nature does not guarantee that every chromosome, gonad, hormone, and organ will align in every person.[1][9][10][11][12]

If the question concerns complete bodily configurations, there is no scientifically privileged list of a few discrete types that captures every variation.[1][9][11][12]

If the question concerns gender identity, there is no universal fixed number.[1][2]

If the question concerns the Swedish population register, there are currently two legal options.[20][21]

The deeper lesson is methodological. Before counting, define the unit. Before classifying a person, state the purpose. Before turning biology into policy, identify the values that biology cannot decide. For this article, following those steps makes the remaining disagreements easier to locate.[1][8]

Animal reproduction usually has two gamete roles, but nature does not always assign one role to one body for life. That distinction is the key to understanding both how common two sexes are and why the animal kingdom contains so many exceptions to the familiar human arrangement.
