The answer depends on the question

What sex actually is in humans and across the animal kingdom, how many sexes there are, and why gamete roles are not the same thing as permanent kinds of individual

August 1, 2026≈ 39 min read✓ Source trail checked

Ask how many sexes there are and the honest answer begins with another question: what are you counting? Across animals, eggs and sperm define two gametic roles, but those roles are distributed among bodies in strikingly different ways. Most described animal species have separate females and males. Many others are simultaneous hermaphrodites, change sex during life, or sometimes reproduce without fertilization. Humans add further questions about bodily traits, identity, and law. The apparent contradiction dissolves when the levels are kept apart.

The short answer

For humans, there are two reproductive sexes in the narrow evolutionary sense: female and male. These correspond to the two kinds of gametes involved in human sexual reproduction, large ova and small sperm. There is no third human gamete type. This definition is standard in a major strand of evolutionary biology, and it explains why the terms female and male can be applied across species whose chromosomes, anatomy, and sex determining mechanisms differ radically.[3][4]

That answer is true, but it is not the whole truth about human bodies. An individual person's sex is ordinarily inferred from several connected traits: chromosomes, gene activity, gonads, internal reproductive structures, external genitalia, hormones, secondary characteristics, and reproductive development. Most people have traits that align in one of two common patterns. Some people have congenital variations in which one or more traits do not follow the usual pattern. The National Academies therefore describes sex as a multidimensional construct made from a cluster of anatomical and physiological traits.[1]

Across the animal kingdom, two gamete classes do not require two permanent classes of individual. Under a common broad classification, about 94 to 95 percent of described animal species are counted as gonochoric, but that proportion is dominated by species rich insects and can include sequential sex changers. Excluding insects, roughly one third of animal species may be hermaphroditic. Some animals perform both roles at once, some change from one to the other, and some reproduce from unfertilized eggs.[23][24]

Gender adds another layer. In current health and social research, gender includes identity, expression, social roles, and cultural expectations. A person's gender identity may be woman, man, nonbinary, or described in other terms. There is no universal scientific catalogue with a fixed number of possible gender identities. Legal systems add still another count. Sweden currently registers two legal sexes, woman and man, even though people may change their registered sex under the law that came into force on 1 July 2025.[20][21]

Gender identityNo fixed universal count. Categories depend partly on language, culture, and self description.

The most defensible answer is not “sex is simply binary” or “sex is simply a spectrum.” It is: reproductive roles are binary in humans, while the traits through which bodies develop and are classified are multidimensional.

One word, several questions

The Swedish word kön is especially overloaded. It can refer to biological sex, gender identity, social gender, legal sex, or even genital anatomy. English separates some of these meanings with the words sex and gender, but the separation is not perfect. Historical usage differs among disciplines, institutions, and countries. A debate can therefore look like a factual disagreement even when its participants are classifying different things.

Consider five questions that are often compressed into “How many sexes are there?”

None of these systems is imaginary. Reproductive biology is real. Congenital variation is real. Gender identity and social treatment have measurable effects on health and life chances. Legal records have practical consequences. Confusion begins when one system is treated as if it automatically answered every question posed in another.

The gamete definition

Sexual reproduction joins genetic material from different cells. In many multicellular organisms, including humans, the gametes differ greatly in size. This is called anisogamy. The larger, resource rich gamete is the ovum. The smaller gamete is the sperm. In evolutionary biology, female is the sex associated with producing the larger gamete, while male is the sex associated with producing the smaller gamete.[3][14]

This definition does useful work. Chromosomes cannot define female and male across all species because nature uses many systems. Mammals commonly use XX and XY chromosomes. Birds use ZW and ZZ, with the female typically heterogametic. In several reptiles, temperature contributes to sex determination. Some fish can change functional sex during life. Plants and animals have repeatedly evolved different genetic switches. Yet when those organisms reproduce anisogamously, the names female and male still track the two gamete roles.[13]

The definition also explains why a third body form is not automatically a third reproductive sex. To establish a third sex in this strict sense, a species would need a third gamete class with a distinct role in reproduction, not simply a third chromosome pattern, appearance, hormone level, or identity. No such third gamete class exists in humans.

Mental imageImagine two biological strategies at fertilization. One invests heavily in each of a smaller number of large cells. The other produces many small cells that compete to reach them. Around those two strategies, evolution builds gonads, ducts, hormones, anatomy, and behavior. The architecture can vary. The two gamete roles remain.

Why two gamete sizes?

The usual evolutionary explanation is disruptive selection. A gamete can gain by becoming larger and provisioning the resulting zygote, or by becoming smaller and increasing its number, mobility, and chance of encounter. An intermediate cell can lose on both fronts: it is less numerous than the smallest cells and provides less material than the largest. Models differ in their details, and anisogamy evolved independently in many lineages, but this tradeoff helps explain why stable systems tend to separate toward two size strategies rather than three or a smooth continuum.[14][24]

This is an explanation of an evolutionary pattern, not a claim that every egg is identical or every sperm is identical. Gametes vary within each class. The distinction concerns two contrasting reproductive strategies and their relative sizes within a species, not a universal measurement threshold shared by elephants, worms, and corals.

Recent scholarship shows that even this apparently simple statement has a boundary. Some authors argue that gamete type supplies the fundamental definition of sex and gives exactly two sexes.[3][4] Others argue that it defines reproductive roles but cannot, by itself, settle every legitimate use of sex in medicine, developmental biology, or human classification.[5][6][7] The disagreement is partly empirical, but also conceptual: must one definition govern every scientific context?

Infertility is not a third sex

A common objection is that babies, postmenopausal women, infertile adults, and many people with differences of sex development do not produce gametes. If sex is defined through gametes, do these people have no sex?

Biologists who use the gametic definition generally classify organisms by their developmental organization toward one of the reproductive roles, not by whether a particular person is fertile at a particular moment. A person can be female without currently producing ova, just as a person can have eyes while being blind. Loss or absence of function does not necessarily erase the kind of organ system that developed.

This reasoning is strongest when the developmental pathway is clear. It becomes less straightforward in rare cases where traits are discordant, gonads are mixed or absent, or development does not resolve toward one typical pathway. Here, an individual classification may require context and clinical detail. That difficulty does not create a new gamete type. It does show why a population level definition and an individual medical description are not interchangeable.

Hermaphroditism provides another test. Many plants and animals can perform both reproductive roles, simultaneously or at different times. A hermaphroditic individual is not necessarily a third sex. It may be one organism capable of functioning as both female and male. In humans, the outdated and stigmatizing term “hermaphrodite” should not be used for people with intersex traits. The relevant clinical terms are intersex variations or differences of sex development, depending on the person and context.[9]

How a body becomes sexed

The slogan “XX is female and XY is male” is a useful school level shorthand, not a complete mechanism. Human sex development is a sequence of interacting processes. Chromosomal material influences gene activity. Gene networks contribute to whether undifferentiated embryonic gonadal tissue develops along testicular or ovarian pathways. Gonads produce hormones. Enzymes convert those hormones. Receptors allow tissues to respond. Internal ducts and external genitalia develop under those signals. Puberty later changes many secondary traits.

A difference at any step can change what follows. Complete androgen insensitivity syndrome offers a clear example. A person may have a 46,XY karyotype and testes that produce androgens, while cells cannot respond normally because of variation in the androgen receptor. External genitalia can be typically female. This is not evidence that chromosomes are unreal. It is evidence that chromosomes act through a developmental system rather than dictating the whole body by themselves.[10]

The converse kind of mismatch can also occur. In 46,XX testicular differences of sex development, a person has two X chromosomes but develops testes and typically a male phenotype. In many cases, a small fragment containing the SRY gene has moved from a Y chromosome during sperm formation. Again, the causal lesson is not that genes do not matter. It is that one chromosome label is only a proxy for a network of genes, signals, tissues, and developmental timing.[12]

Congenital adrenal hyperplasia provides a different route. In common severe forms, altered steroid synthesis can expose a 46,XX fetus to elevated androgens and produce varying degrees of masculinization of the external genitalia. Some forms can also cause dangerous salt loss and require prompt medical care. A biological variation can therefore be both part of human diversity and clinically important. Respectful language should never become an excuse to ignore a health risk.[11]

Mental imageDo not picture sex development as a single switch marked XX or XY. Picture a branching river. Chromosomes shape the headwaters, but genes, gonads, enzymes, hormones, receptors, anatomy, and puberty form successive channels. Most rivers reach one of two broad deltas. A minority follow less common channels.

Intersex variations

Intersex is an umbrella term for congenital variations in sex characteristics that do not all fit typical definitions of female or male bodies. Differences of sex development, often shortened to DSD, is common in clinical literature. Some people prefer intersex, some prefer DSD, and some prefer the name of a specific diagnosis. Terminology has a history of stigma, so the person's own preference matters.

Variations can involve chromosomes, gonads, hormone synthesis, hormone response, internal anatomy, external genitalia, or combinations of traits. They do not all have the same medical implications or lived experience. Turner syndrome, Klinefelter syndrome, androgen insensitivity, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, ovotesticular DSD, and many other conditions differ profoundly. Treating them as one third biological sex obscures more than it explains.

How common are intersex traits? The answer depends heavily on the definition. A widely repeated estimate is about 1.7 percent, but it groups a broad set of variations, including some that do not produce ambiguous genitalia and may never challenge ordinary sex assignment. A much narrower estimate focused on conditions involving discordance between chromosomal sex and phenotypic sex has been reported at roughly 0.018 percent.[15][16] Clinical estimates for visibly atypical genital development at birth often fall between these extremes. There is no honest single prevalence figure without stating what was counted.

This numerical dispute teaches a general lesson. Categories do not merely discover totals. Their inclusion rules help produce the total. That is not scientific fraud. It is why good science publishes definitions and sensitivity analyses rather than presenting a politically convenient number as if it descended from nature fully formed.

Intersex variations neither prove that reproductive roles are unlimited nor disappear because human reproduction uses two gametes. They show that the mapping from reproductive roles to whole individual bodies is not perfectly one to one. Any account that denies either part is incomplete.

Binary, bimodal, or spectrum?

Three words dominate public discussion: binary, bimodal, and spectrum. Each can be useful. Each can also mislead.

Binary

A variable is binary if it has two possible categories. Human gamete type is binary: ova or sperm. Reproductive role is therefore binary in this narrow framework. But chromosomes are not simply two values, because karyotypes such as XXY, X0, XXX, mosaics, and chimeras occur. Hormone concentrations are quantitative. Genital anatomy and secondary traits vary. Calling “all aspects of sex” binary silently changes a precise claim into a much broader one.

Bimodal

A distribution is bimodal when observations cluster around two common regions. Many human sex traits are strongly bimodal. Adult testosterone concentrations, gonadal anatomy, and several secondary characteristics usually fall into two overlapping or nearly separate distributions. Bimodal does not mean that every value lies at one of two points. It means two modes dominate the distribution.

Spectrum

Spectrum language correctly emphasizes variation and can be humane and useful in discussing multidimensional sex traits. Yet it can suggest that every trait varies along one common line from female to male. Biology is more complicated. A person can have one combination of chromosomes, gonads, receptors, anatomy, and hormones that cannot be represented faithfully by a single point on a ruler. A map with several dimensions is often better than a spectrum.

This is why the National Academies and several epidemiologists recommend measuring the specific dimensions relevant to the research question rather than asking one vague sex or gender item to stand in for all of them.[1][8]

A careful formulation: Human reproductive sex is binary at the level of gamete class. Human sex traits are multidimensional, strongly clustered into two common patterns, and not universally concordant. “Binary” and “spectrum” are therefore answers to different models, not magic words that settle every question.

Why experts still disagree

It may seem surprising that biologists, physicians, philosophers, and social scientists can inspect much of the same evidence and still disagree about whether sex is binary. The disagreement does not usually concern the existence of ova and sperm, or the existence of intersex variations. It concerns what a scientific category is supposed to do.

One approach seeks a definition that is stable across the tree of life. Gamete type performs well. It lets an evolutionary biologist compare a flowering plant, a fish, and a mammal without pretending that all three use human chromosomes or anatomy. It also connects the categories female and male to a powerful theory of anisogamy, sexual selection, parental investment, and reproductive conflict. From this perspective, chromosomes and bodies are mechanisms and manifestations of sex, while gamete role is sex itself.[3][4]

A second approach begins with human development and medicine. Here, saying that a patient is “male” may be intended to communicate something about hormones, organs, expected laboratory ranges, disease risks, or drug responses. Gamete role may not be the mechanism under investigation. A person who has had gonads removed, takes exogenous hormones, or has an androgen receptor variation can differ medically from another person assigned to the same reproductive class. Researchers in this tradition argue that sex is better understood as several partly connected variables.[5][8]

A third approach asks how categories are made and used. A definition can be biologically coherent yet still fail to justify a legal rule. Conversely, an administrative category can be useful without constituting a newly discovered fact of reproductive biology. Scholars working in philosophy, ethics, law, or science studies therefore examine not only whether a classification tracks nature, but why that feature was selected, whose needs the classification serves, and what consequences follow.[6][7]

These approaches overlap, but they optimize for different virtues:

Scientific categories are tools. “Species,” “gene,” “intelligence,” and “disease” also have multiple definitions because researchers need them for different explanatory tasks. This does not mean that anything goes. A definition must be explicit, internally coherent, supported by evidence, and suitable for its purpose. It also must not slide unnoticed from one purpose to another.

The phrase “there is no consensus” requires care as well. It does not mean that every claim has equal standing. There is broad agreement that humans reproduce with ova and sperm, that most sex traits cluster in two common patterns, that congenital variations occur, and that sex and gender identity should not be treated as synonyms. The lack of consensus concerns whether one level has exclusive authority to define the word sex in every setting. That narrower but important disagreement is visible in peer reviewed exchanges published as recently as 2025 and 2026.[3][4][6][7]

Political debate often erases this structure. One side may cite anisogamy as if it answered how a hospital should record every relevant trait. Another may cite developmental variation as if it created additional gamete classes. Both moves take a valid observation out of the scale where it does its best work.

A census of the animal kingdom

Humans are a poor sample of animal reproductive diversity. We are mammals, and mammals overwhelmingly package female and male function into separate individuals for life. Look across the animal kingdom and the same two gamete roles remain widespread, but the ways they are assigned to bodies multiply.

The technical term for a species with separate female and male individuals is gonochoric. A widely cited broad estimate from comparative syntheses classifies about 94 to 95 percent of described animal species as gonochoric and roughly 5 to 6 percent as hermaphroditic.[23][24] These are species weighted estimates from an incomplete and taxonomically uneven record, not direct measurements of every animal species. At first sight they nevertheless seem decisive: separate sexes are nearly universal among described animals.

Classification rules matter. In one influential macroevolutionary analysis, a species was coded as gonochoric if it had separate sexes at any point in its sexually active life cycle. Sequential hermaphrodites were therefore included as gonochoric because each individual performs one role during a given adult phase.[23] Other zoological accounts group those same species with hermaphrodites because one individual performs both roles over a lifetime. The 94 to 95 percent estimate is consequently not an exact count of species with two lifelong, unchanging kinds of individual.

But the headline percentage hides a huge sampling effect. Insects make up an enormous share of described animal species, and insect species are almost entirely gonochoric. Remove insects from the calculation and hermaphroditism is estimated to occur in about one third of animal species.[23][25] In other words, the answer depends not only on what we count, but also on whether every species, every lineage, or every individual receives equal weight.

These figures are informed estimates, not a finished planetary census. More than a million animal species have been described, many only from preserved specimens or a small number of observations. Reproductive systems can be misclassified when sex change is brief, when gonads are not examined, or when a species is assumed to resemble its relatives. The undiscovered animal diversity is also not a random sample. A percentage based on described species therefore measures the state of taxonomy as well as nature.

Common among species, widespread among lineages

Species frequency is not the same as evolutionary breadth. Hermaphroditism may account for only about one species in twenty when insects are included, yet reviews have recorded it in more than 70 percent of animal phyla, including 24 of 34 phyla in one commonly used classification. The exact denominator changes with taxonomy, but the pattern has appeared in distant branches of the animal tree rather than arising as one isolated curiosity.[23][34] Counting species makes insects numerically dominant. Counting major lineages makes the repeated reach of hermaphroditism much more visible.

Nor can the present day majority reveal the original animal condition. Reconstructions indicate repeated transitions between gonochorism and hermaphroditism, with some analyses finding more transitions toward hermaphroditism than away from it. The sexual system of the last common ancestor of living animals remains unresolved because different models and branch length assumptions give different answers.[23] Three questions must therefore be kept separate: which system contains the most living species, how often systems have changed, and which state was ancestral.

How do researchers recognize functional hermaphroditism?

Anatomy alone can mislead. Ovarian and testicular tissue may coexist during juvenile development, a transition between sexes, or a rare nonfunctional variation. In fishes, diagnostic reviews look for functional evidence such as observed spawning in both roles, mature ovarian and testicular tissue, or a documented histological sequence through sex change.[39] This stricter standard is one reason older species lists are revised rather than merely enlarged.

Even so, the broad pattern is robust. Separate females and males dominate insects and vertebrates. Hermaphroditism is much more prominent in numerous invertebrate groups, including many flatworms, annelids, molluscs, sponges, tunicates, corals, barnacles, and other animals whose lives differ radically from ours.[24][26]

Two gametic roles are common across animals. Two separate, fixed kinds of individual are also very common, but they are not a universal requirement of animal sexual reproduction.

One body, both roles

A simultaneous hermaphrodite can produce both eggs and sperm during the same phase of life. Earthworms, many snails, numerous flatworms, and many sessile marine animals follow some version of this pattern. This does not usually mean that each animal simply fertilizes itself. Many exchange sperm with partners, and some possess behavioral or physiological barriers that reduce self fertilization.

One important advantage is easiest to see when encounters are uncertain. A mobile mammal of one sex may search for a member of the other. A slow snail, a burrowing worm, or an animal fixed to a reef cannot assume that the next conspecific it encounters will be the right sex. If every mature individual can mate in either role, every encounter is potentially reproductive. That advantage can be especially powerful at low population density or when dispersal is limited.[25][26]

But mate scarcity is not a universal master explanation. Body size, fertilization mode, latitude, mobility, brooding, local sperm competition, mating group size, phylogenetic inheritance, and tradeoffs between male and female function can all matter. Comparative studies find different associations in different animal groups, and several classic predictions remain incompletely tested.[26][34] Hermaphroditism is a family of evolutionary solutions, not one adaptation with one cause.

Self fertilization is another possibility, but hermaphroditism and selfing are not synonyms. Selfing can provide reproductive insurance when no partner is available, while also increasing homozygosity and exposing harmful recessive variants. A wide comparative review found that self fertilization is distributed very unevenly among hermaphroditic animals, reflecting a balance among mate availability, inbreeding costs, ecology, and life history.[25]

There are also mixed systems. In androdioecious species, males coexist with hermaphrodites. In gynodioecious species, females coexist with hermaphrodites. A few populations contain females, males, and hermaphrodites, sometimes called trioecy. That can create three reproductive categories of individual within one population. Yet the gametes involved are still eggs and sperm. This is a perfect example of why “How many sexes?” needs a unit of counting.

More forms do not mean more sexes

Animals of the same sex can look and behave radically differently. Territorial males, sneaker males, female mimics, and other alternative reproductive phenotypes may use different routes to fertilization while all producing sperm. Evolutionary biologists therefore describe them as tactics or morphs within a sex, not additional gamete defined sexes.[31]

Social insects make the distinction even sharper. Queens and workers can develop from closely similar genomes, yet follow different developmental paths. Queens usually reproduce, while workers may be partly or completely sterile and can diversify into behavioral or anatomical subcastes. These are divisions of labor among females, not new gamete classes.[40] Appearance, fertility, caste, and sex are related in particular species but are not interchangeable counting systems.

Two sexes do not mean fifty fifty

The number of sexes says nothing by itself about their numerical balance. A gonochoric species still has two sexes if a local population is 90 percent female. Researchers distinguish the sex ratio among offspring, the ratio among adults, and the operational sex ratio among individuals currently ready to mate. Differential mortality, dispersal, environmental sex determination, local competition, and conflicts among genetic elements can move those ratios far from equality.[33]

The familiar expectation of a balanced sex ratio is also often misstated. Under particular assumptions, selection favors equal parental investment in sons and daughters, not necessarily equal numbers at every age or place. If one sex costs more to produce, equal investment may generate unequal counts.

In simultaneous hermaphrodites, counting females and males may not even be the relevant problem. Sex allocation theory instead asks how an individual divides limited reproductive resources between egg production and sperm production. That allocation can shift with body size, number of mates, sperm competition, and brooding costs.[34] Thus “How many sexes exist?”, “How many individuals of each sex are present?”, and “How much is invested in each sex function?” are three different questions.

Changing sex

Sequential hermaphrodites perform one reproductive role and later another. Protandry means male first and female later. Clownfish are the famous example: within a social group, the largest fish is the breeding female, the next largest is the breeding male, and a change in hierarchy can trigger the male to become female. Protogyny means female first and male later, a pattern found in many wrasses and groupers. Some fishes can change in either direction.[27]

Why change at all? The classic size advantage model predicts sex change when reproductive success rises with body size more steeply in one role than in the other. In a harem forming fish, for example, a large male may monopolize many matings while a small male gains little. Starting as female and changing to male after growing large can then yield more descendants. Where larger females gain disproportionately from producing more eggs, selection can favor the reverse sequence.

Sex change is not a superficial relabeling. Gonadal tissue reorganizes, hormone systems shift, gene expression changes, and behavior can alter rapidly. Social information can be causal. Removing a dominant individual may change the status of another animal and initiate a coordinated biological transition. A review of natural sex change in fishes documents genetic, neuroendocrine, gonadal, anatomical, and behavioral changes across this process.[27]

A phylogenetic study of 4,614 teleost fish species included 4,320 classified as gonochoric, 196 protogynous, 36 protandrous, 16 capable of bidirectional change, and 46 simultaneous hermaphrodites. The analysis found gonochorism to be the likely ancestral and an evolutionarily stable state in teleosts, while transitions among systems have occurred repeatedly.[28] This sample is not a census of all fishes, but it shows both sides of the story: fixed separate sexes dominate, while alternative systems are real, diverse, and evolutionarily recurrent.

Fish supply the best studied examples, but sex change is not a fish invention. Slipper snails in the genus Crepidula mature first as males and later become females. Several caridean shrimps are protandrous, while species of Lysmata begin with a male phase and later add female function without losing the capacity to function as male.[24][32] Sequential systems also occur in other molluscs, annelids, crustaceans, and additional invertebrate lineages. The details differ, but the logic is the same: a life can occupy two established reproductive roles without creating a third gamete type.

Reproduction without males

Some animals can produce offspring from eggs without fertilization. This is parthenogenesis. It has evolved in insects, crustaceans, rotifers, nematodes, fishes, and reptiles. Some lineages use it obligately, some alternate sexual and asexual generations, and others do it facultatively under particular conditions.

Parthenogenesis does not create a third gamete. It removes the immediate need for sperm. An obligately parthenogenetic lineage may currently contain eggs but no sperm at all. Its members are conventionally called female because they produce ova or egg derived propagules homologous to those of females in their sexual relatives, not because two gamete classes are secretly present in the lineage.[35] The number of sexes represented in a population, the number of gamete classes currently produced, the system in its sexual relatives, and the number of parents required for a particular birth can therefore differ.

It is rare when animals are counted as a whole and exceptionally rare among vertebrates. A broad review estimated obligate parthenogenesis below 0.1 percent of vertebrate species. In certain arthropod groups it is much more common. One systematic survey documented 765 obligately parthenogenetic species across nine haplodiploid arthropod orders, while stressing that discovery and reporting are uneven.[29]

There are also systems that resist ordinary intuition. Aphids may cycle between many parthenogenetic generations and one sexual generation. In haplodiploid bees, ants, and wasps, unfertilized eggs commonly develop into haploid males and fertilized eggs into diploid females, although the details vary. Whiptail lizards include all female species that reproduce without males. These are not extra sexes. They are alternative rules for turning eggs, genomes, and environments into offspring.

When an asexual lineage still needs sperm

The boundary between sex and asexuality is not perfectly clean. In gynogenesis, an egg needs contact with sperm to begin development, but the sperm genome is normally excluded, so the offspring inherit their nuclear genome from the mother. In hybridogenesis, part of a hybrid genome is discarded before gamete production and restored through mating in the next generation. Kleptogenetic systems may incorporate paternal genetic material only sometimes.[35]

These systems separate four events that ordinary reproduction bundles together: mating, egg activation, genetic contribution, and parenthood. A population may contain only females of one lineage while depending on sperm from males of a related sexual species. Sperm can therefore be ecologically essential without supplying the offspring's usual nuclear inheritance. Again, the gametes are still eggs and sperm, but the rules governing what each contributes have changed.

Many switches, two roles

One of the great lessons of comparative biology is that the mechanism deciding sex is far less conserved than the reproductive outcomes it produces. Humans and many other mammals commonly use an XX and XY system. Birds and many snakes use ZZ and ZW. In haplodiploid insects, chromosome set number matters. In crocodilians and many turtles, incubation temperature influences sex. In some fishes and invertebrates, several genes and the environment interact. In still others, social context helps trigger sex change.

A major comparative review described this as a paradox: the upstream switches evolve rapidly and differ bewilderingly among lineages, while the downstream distinction between producing relatively large eggs and relatively small sperm is strongly conserved.[24] The existence of many sex determining mechanisms therefore does not imply many gametic sexes. It implies many evolutionary routes to two reproductive roles, plus many ways of placing those roles in one body, two bodies, or one body at different times.

Even one named species need not use a single mechanism everywhere. Geographically separated populations of the Japanese wrinkled frog Rana rugosa use both XX and XY systems and ZZ and ZW systems, with mixed populations near a contact zone.[38] In fishes, sexual pattern itself can also vary among populations as density and mating conditions change.[30] A species label is therefore sometimes a useful summary rather than an exceptionless rule.

When another organism reaches into the switch

Sex determination is not always controlled only by an animal's own genome and physical environment. Maternally inherited Wolbachia bacteria infect many arthropods and can feminize genetic males, induce parthenogenesis, kill male embryos, or create incompatibility between sperm and eggs. These manipulations favor the transmission of bacteria through eggs.[36]

Wolbachia does not invent another gamete class. It changes which hosts develop, which survive, and whether sexual reproduction succeeds. The example reveals that a sex determining system can be an ecological interaction among genomes, rather than a sealed program located entirely inside one animal.

Mental imageThink of animal sex as a stage play with two recurring reproductive roles, egg producer and sperm producer. Evolution keeps rewriting the casting rules. Sometimes each actor receives one role for life. Sometimes every actor can play both. Sometimes the role changes with size, temperature, or social rank. Sometimes one role temporarily performs the scene alone.

Which individual are we counting?

For a solitary vertebrate, “one animal” usually seems obvious. Modular and colonial animals make the unit itself part of the problem. A coral, bryozoan, or siphonophore can consist of repeated modules, often called polyps or zooids, produced by budding. Researchers may describe sex at the level of a module, a colony, or the whole genetic individual. A colony containing egg producing and sperm producing modules can be functionally hermaphroditic even when each reproductive module performs only one role.

Life cycles add another layer. Many cnidarians alternate a sessile polyp phase with a mobile medusa phase, and sexual differentiation can be expressed differently across those stages. Some polyps multiply by budding and later produce medusae that make gametes. Asexual budding creates new modules from body tissue; parthenogenesis develops an egg without fertilization. They are distinct processes even though neither requires the ordinary fusion of egg and sperm at that moment.[37]

This is not merely exotic terminology. It exposes a hidden assumption in every percentage. Before calling a species gonochoric or hermaphroditic, a researcher must decide which stage and which level of biological individuality carries the reproductive functions. Different defensible units can yield different descriptions of the same living system.

Beyond animals

The picture becomes stranger outside the animal kingdom. Some unicellular organisms are isogamous, meaning their fusing cells or gametes are similar in size. Under a definition based on anisogamous gamete size, male and female do not apply to a strictly isogamous system. That does not make the system sexless in every broader genetic sense. It means that meiotic recombination and cell fusion occur without the large egg and small sperm distinction.

Mating types answer a separate compatibility question: which cells, nuclei, or hyphae can fuse? Fungi can have many alleles at one or more compatibility loci, generating thousands of possible mating type combinations even when the partners are not differentiated into eggs and sperm. Some fungi fuse hyphae rather than free swimming gametes. Number of mating types and number of anisogamous sexes are therefore not rival answers to one count.[18][24]

The ciliate Tetrahymena thermophila, for example, has seven mating types, and cells can pair with a different type.[17] Popular accounts sometimes call these “seven sexes.” That can be useful shorthand if sex means a compatibility class, but it is misleading if readers infer seven gamete sizes or seven reproductive roles. The underlying biology is uncontroversial. The label depends on which definition is being used.

Sex, gender, identity, and orientation

Gender is not merely a polite synonym for sex. The World Health Organization distinguishes sex related biological characteristics from gender as socially constructed norms, roles, and relations. Gender identity is a person's internal experience of gender and may or may not correspond to assigned sex.[2]

A transgender woman is a woman whose gender identity differs from the male sex assigned at birth. A transgender man is a man whose gender identity differs from the female sex assigned at birth. Nonbinary is an umbrella term for identities that are not exclusively woman or man. These statements classify identity. They do not claim that a new human gamete has been discovered.

How many genders are there? Science cannot supply a timeless integer because gender categories are partly social, cultural, linguistic, and personal. Researchers can count the response options in a particular survey, the identities reported in a particular population, or the legal markers available in a particular state. They cannot infer from those counts a final inventory for every culture and time.

That does not make gender unreal. Money, citizenship, language, kinship, and law are also socially organized, yet they produce observable consequences. Gender norms influence work, violence, status, clothing, caregiving, medical treatment, and health. Identity can be measured, although measurement must state what dimension it captures. The National Academies explicitly treats gender as multidimensional, linking identity, expression, and social expectations.[1]

Nor are all nonbinary terms interchangeable. Hijra, Two Spirit, and other culturally specific categories emerge from distinct histories and communities. Removing them from context and presenting them as items in a universal Western list can be another kind of simplification.

Sexual orientation is another dimension

Sexual orientation is not a synonym for sex or gender identity. It describes patterns of sexual, romantic, or emotional attraction and may also be measured through identity and behavior. Those dimensions do not always align perfectly with one another.[19] A person can therefore have a biological sex profile, a gender identity, and a sexual orientation without any one of them supplying the value of the others.

Words such as heterosexual, gay, lesbian, bisexual, pansexual, and asexual do not name additional biological sexes. They describe orientation or its absence using a vocabulary that often refers to one's own gender and that of potential partners. Keeping orientation explicit prevents another common counting error: treating every category in the acronym LGBTQI+ as if it were the same kind of category.

Legal sex

Legal sex is a state classification. It governs documents, population registers, and sometimes rules that depend on sex or gender. Because law serves administrative and political purposes, its categories need not map perfectly onto gametes, anatomy, or identity.

As of August 2026, Sweden has two legal sexes in the population register, woman and man. Since 1 July 2025, the medical process and the legal process are separated more clearly, and a person aged 16 or older may apply to change legal sex if statutory conditions are met. Socialstyrelsen states explicitly that Sweden still has only those two registered categories.[20][21]

Other jurisdictions permit an X marker, a third category, or the omission of a sex marker in some documents. These are legal responses to identity, privacy, and administrative needs. Their existence does not alter the number of gamete types. Conversely, a country with only two legal markers does not erase people whose identity is nonbinary or whose sex traits are intersex.

Human rights do not depend on winning a taxonomy debate. A person does not need to constitute a third reproductive sex to deserve bodily autonomy, accurate health care, protection from discrimination, or documents that allow ordinary life. Recent scholarly disagreement is especially clear on this point: biological definitions should not be smuggled into moral and legal conclusions without a separate argument.[6][7][22]

Eight claims put to the test

“Sex is determined only by chromosomes”

Verdict: false. Chromosomes are important upstream factors, but development depends on particular genes, gene regulation, gonads, hormone synthesis, enzymes, receptors, anatomy, and timing. A 46,XY person with complete androgen insensitivity may have typically female external anatomy. A 46,XX person with SRY material can develop testes. Chromosomes are powerful evidence, not a complete description of a body.[10][12]

“There are only XX women and XY men”

Verdict: false as a universal claim. Most women are 46,XX and most men are 46,XY, but exceptions include sex chromosome aneuploidies, mosaics, chimeras, androgen response differences, and gene translocations. A rule can be highly predictive without being exceptionless.

“Intersex is a third sex”

Verdict: usually misleading. Intersex is an umbrella over many distinct variations. People with these traits are not one homogeneous reproductive class, do not share one anatomy, and do not produce a shared third gamete. Some people do identify their sex as intersex, and that identity should be respected. That personal or legal usage is different from claiming a third anisogamous reproductive role.

“Intersex proves that sex is not binary”

Verdict: definition dependent. Intersex variations do not overturn the two gamete model. They do show that chromosome, gonadal, hormonal, genital, and secondary traits are not each strictly binary and perfectly aligned in every person. Thus the claim is false if “sex” means gamete role, but it identifies a real limitation if “sex” means a complete individual trait profile.

“Sex is a spectrum”

Verdict: useful but incomplete. Many traits vary quantitatively, and sex development can produce combinations outside typical patterns. Yet no single female to male line captures all dimensions. “Multidimensional and bimodally clustered” is often more precise. Spectrum language is best treated as a corrective to rigid trait essentialism, not as a replacement for all categorical biology.

“Gender is purely a personal feeling”

Verdict: too narrow. Gender identity includes an internal dimension, but gender also refers to social roles, norms, expression, institutions, and how others classify a person. Those processes affect material outcomes. The fact that categories are socially organized does not make their effects unreal.[1][2]

“If gender has many categories, biology must have the same number”

Verdict: false. Categories at different levels need not match one for one. Languages can have many colour words although the electromagnetic spectrum is continuous. States can have several residence statuses although human migration is a physical movement. Gender vocabularies classify identity and social position. Gamete categories classify reproductive role.

“Biology settles the legal and moral question”

Verdict: false. Biology can identify consequences, constraints, and relevant differences. It cannot decide by itself which differences a state should record, when privacy should prevail, what fairness requires, or how competing rights should be balanced. Those conclusions require ethical and political premises. Pretending otherwise hides value judgments inside scientific language.

The right variable for the question

The best classification is the one that answers the actual question while collecting no more sensitive information than necessary.

This context sensitive approach avoids two symmetrical mistakes. The first is biological reductionism, where chromosomes are assumed to answer every medical, social, and legal question. The second is biological erasure, where respect for identity is treated as a reason not to measure anatomy or physiology when they are causally relevant.

Precision medicine should ask about the mechanism it needs. Respectful social interaction should generally use the person's name and identity. Sports, prisons, statistics, and clinical guidelines raise harder policy questions because they combine bodies, risk, fairness, privacy, and rights. Biology can inform those choices. It cannot make all the value judgments for us.

A better final answer

What, then, is sex? It is both a reproductive distinction and, in individual organisms, a developmental organization expressed through multiple traits. Across anisogamous animals, eggs and sperm define two reproductive roles. Nature then distributes those roles in several ways: between fixed females and males, within simultaneous hermaphrodites, across successive phases of a sex changing life, or alongside episodes of reproduction without fertilization.

How common are two sexes? If this means two gamete classes, they are the defining pattern of anisogamous animal reproduction. If it means species broadly classified as gonochoric, a widely cited estimate is about 94 to 95 percent of described animal species, but only about two thirds after insects are removed from the count. The estimate depends partly on whether sequential sex changers are coded by their role at one time or across a lifetime. The remainder includes a remarkable diversity of simultaneous hermaphroditic and mixed systems. Two roles are more universal than two fixed kinds of body.

For humans, traits usually cohere around two common reproductive pathways, but nature does not guarantee that every chromosome, gonad, hormone, organ, and identity will align in every person. If the question concerns complete bodily configurations, there is no scientifically privileged list of a few discrete types that captures every variation. If it concerns gender identity, there is no universal fixed number. If it concerns the Swedish population register, there are currently two legal options.

The deeper lesson is methodological. Before counting, define the unit. Before classifying a person, state the purpose. Before turning biology into policy, identify the values that biology cannot decide. Once those steps are taken, much of the loudest disagreement becomes tractable.

Animal reproduction usually has two gamete roles, but nature does not always assign one role to one body for life. That distinction is the key to understanding both how common two sexes are and why the animal kingdom contains so many exceptions to the familiar human arrangement.
